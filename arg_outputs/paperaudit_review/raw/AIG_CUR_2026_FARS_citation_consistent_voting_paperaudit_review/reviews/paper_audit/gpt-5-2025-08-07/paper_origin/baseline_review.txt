1) Summary
   - The paper addresses permutation sensitivity in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), where answer quality depends on the order of retrieved documents (Introduction, Section 1; Related Work, Sections 2.1–2.4). It proposes Citation-Consistent Voting (CCV), a training-free, inference-time aggregation method that prompts the LLM to produce structured citations, filters outputs for “evidence validity,” maps cited document positions to permutation-invariant document IDs, and selects answers maximizing citation-ID agreement across K permutations (Method, Sections 3.2–3.3; Equations (1)–(2); Figure 1). On NaturalQuestions with Contriever Top-5 and Qwen3-8B, CCV achieves 46.37% SubEM and 46.83% F1 at K=20, improving over majority voting by +0.19 SubEM and +0.17 F1 (Experiments, Table 1, Section 4.2). The improvement increases with K (Table 2; Figure 2), and citation agreement correlates with correctness (mean 3.04 vs. 2.85; Mann–Whitney p=1.14×10^-5) (Section 4.5). The method requires no training and is claimed to be broadly compatible (Abstract; Method, Section 3.4).2) Strengths
   - Bolded titles with sub-point examples- Evidence-grounded aggregation via citation IDs
     - The core aggregation signal uses permutation-invariant document IDs mapped from cited indices (Method, Section 3.3; Equations (1)–(2)), improving robustness by targeting a more stable signal than raw answer strings (novelty and conceptual clarity).
     - Figure 1 explicitly operationalizes the three stages, including mapping “doc_idx → Underlying Doc ID” before grouping and scoring by answer (Figure 1), aiding reproducibility and clarity (presentation strength).
     - The diagnostic shows higher agreement scores for correct predictions (3.04) vs. incorrect (2.85), p=1.14×10^-5 (Experiments, Section 4.5), empirically supporting the premise that citation consistency is meaningful (empirical validation).- Training-free, plug-and-play design
     - The approach does not require model updates and is positioned as compatible “with any RAG system that can produce structured citations” (Abstract; Introduction, bullet 1), enhancing practicality and deployment potential (impact).
     - Computational overhead is limited to K forward passes, with minimal extra cost for filtering/aggregation and compatibility with batched inference (Method, Section 3.4), suggesting feasibility (technical practicality).
     - JSON-constrained outputs with citations/quotes are integrated into the pipeline (Method, Section 3.2; Figure 1), making incorporation into modern RAG prompts straightforward (clarity and usability).- Clear mathematical formalization
     - The mapping from cited index to underlying document ID and the modal-ID voting rule are precisely defined (Method, Equations (1)–(2)), reducing ambiguity (technical soundness).
     - The problem setup formally defines the permutation ensemble setting (Method, Section 3.1), aligning with standard RAG permutation self-consistency framing (clarity).
     - Tie-breaking by raw answer frequency is specified (Method, Section 3.3), making the selection rule complete (reproducibility).- Consistent K-scaling trend
     - Table 2 and Figure 2 show that Δ(SubEM) vs. majority voting increases with K: -0.01 (K=5), +0.10 (K=10), +0.11 (K=15), +0.19 (K=20) (Experiments, Sections 4.3–4.4 captions), indicating the aggregation signal strengthens with more permutations (empirical pattern).
     - The narrative in Section 4.3 explains the intuition: larger K provides more reliable citation agreement signals (analysis clarity).
     - The K-scaling analysis isolates the effect on SubEM and provides a simple trend visualization (Figure 2), improving interpretability (presentation).- Empirical improvement under a controlled JSON-citation format
     - Within the JSON-citation regime, CCV outperforms majority voting on both SubEM (46.37 vs. 46.18) and F1 (46.83 vs. 46.66) at K=20 (Table 1; Section 4.2), supporting the claimed benefit (empirical support).
     - The comparison to “Vanilla RAG (JSON Citation)” ensures all multi-permutation methods share the same constrained-output setting (Table 1; Section 4.2), helping isolate aggregation effects (experimental control).
     - Reporting both SubEM and F1 (Table 1; Section 4.2) provides a more complete performance picture (evaluation rigor).- Helpful ablation on quote verification
     - Table 3 shows that relaxing verbatim quote matching increases coverage (96.37% → 99.70%) and valid runs (4.12 → 4.96), lifting SubEM at K=5 from 45.73% to 46.37% (Experiments, Section 4.4), highlighting a practical trade-off (analysis depth).
     - The ablation directly measures coverage/valid runs, providing interpretable diagnostics beyond accuracy (Table 3), useful for engineering decisions (practical relevance).
     - The text links the gain to improved citation-path coverage (Section 4.4), offering a plausible mechanism (explanatory clarity).- Transparent acknowledgement of limitations
     - The conclusion notes modest absolute gains, single dataset/model evaluation, and the K-forward-pass cost (Conclusion, Section 5), enabling a balanced interpretation (honesty and positioning).
     - The paper scopes future directions (e.g., combining with training-based methods, broader datasets) (Conclusion, Section 5), indicating awareness of broader landscape (roadmap clarity).
     - The dataset, retriever, and model are clearly stated (NaturalQuestions; Contriever Top-5; Qwen3-8B; greedy decoding) (Experiments, Section 4.1), aiding reproducibility (experimental transparency).3) Weaknesses
   - Bolded titles with sub-point examples- Limited empirical scope and modest effect sizes
     - Only one dataset (NaturalQuestions) and one model-retriever pair (Qwen3-8B + Contriever Top-5) are evaluated (Experiments, Section 4.1), limiting external validity (generalization).
     - The absolute gains are small: +0.19 SubEM and +0.17 F1 at K=20 (Table 1; Section 4.2), which may be within typical variance for such setups (impact).
     - While K-scaling shows monotonic Δ(SubEM), the absolute differences remain ≤0.19 even at K=20 (Table 2; Figure 2), suggesting limited practical headroom demonstrated so far (practical significance).- Incomplete methodological details impeding reproducibility
     - The exact prompt(s) for JSON-constrained citation outputs are not provided (Method, Section 3.2; Figure 1 mentions “JSON-constrained generation” but no template), hindering faithful replication (reproducibility).
     - Evidence filtering specifics (e.g., string normalization, quote matching tolerance, case handling) are unspecified beyond an “exact substring” check and a “no-quote” variant (Method, Section 3.2; Section 3.4), leaving key implementation choices ambiguous (technical clarity).
     - Permutation sampling details (e.g., uniqueness of permutations, random seed control, across-run variance reporting) are not described; only one baseline row has ± values (Table 1, “MoA Majority Vote K=5: 45.82±0.28 / 46.38±0.21”), with no analogous uncertainty for CCV (Experiments, Sections 4.1–4.3), limiting interpretability of significance (experimental rigor).- Evidence-relaxation risks undermining grounding claims
     - The “no-quote” variant treats any in-range doc index as evidence-valid (Method, Section 3.4; Experiments, Table 3), allowing unverifiable or spurious citations to pass filtering (methodological soundness).
     - The pipeline diagram emphasizes an “Evidence Validity Check” that requires the quote to be in the cited doc and answer in quote (Figure 1 caption; Method, Section 3.2), but the best-reported results favor relaxing this check (Table 3), creating a mismatch between conceptual framing and practice (consistency).
     - There is no measurement of citation accuracy (e.g., fraction of citations that truly support the answer) or robustness to adversarial/misattributed citations when the quote constraint is dropped (Experiments, Section 4.5 focuses on agreement vs. correctness; No direct evidence found in the manuscript), leaving the safety of the relaxation unquantified (risk assessment).- Baselines and comparison framing could be stronger
     - The “Vanilla RAG (Standard)” prompt yields higher SubEM but lower F1 than the JSON-citation prompt (Table 1; Section 4.2), complicating cross-row comparisons and raising format-induced confounds (fairness).
     - The baseline labeled “Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) Majority Vote” (Table 1; Related Work, Section 2.3) conflates a multi-agent method with permutation ensembling via majority vote, potentially confusing readers about what is actually compared (terminology precision).
     - No alternative inference-time baselines beyond answer-frequency voting are included (e.g., retrieval-score-weighted voting or citation-frequency voting without ID mapping); No direct evidence found in the manuscript, leaving unclear whether CCV’s advantage is specific to ID agreement vs. other simple signals (completeness).- Analysis depth is limited
     - The diagnostic shows a statistically significant difference in mean agreement (3.04 vs. 2.85; p=1.14×10^-5) (Section 4.5), but no effect-size metrics (e.g., AUC, Cohen’s d) or calibration curves are provided, making practical separability unclear (interpretability).
     - No qualitative error cases or counterexamples illustrate when citation consistency helps or fails; No direct evidence found in the manuscript (analysis richness).
     - Sensitivity to retrieval depth (Top-5 only) and to K beyond 20 or to temperature/sampling remains unexplored (Experiments, Section 4.1 uses Top-5, T=0; Section 4.3 varies only K), limiting understanding of operating regimes (completeness).- Clarity and presentation issues
     - The manuscript contains repeated “WARNING: This paper was generated by an automated research system” and stray tokens like “ANALEMMA” (Abstract; Introduction footnote block; Method figure block; Experiments block), which distract from the scientific content (polish).
     - The nomenclature “MoA Majority Vote” (Table 1; Related Work, Section 2.3) may mislead readers to think a multi-agent system was used, rather than permutation-based majority voting (clarity).
     - The practical considerations mention KV-cache sharing across permutations with shared prefixes (Method, Section 3.4) without clarifying how often such shared prefixes occur under random permutations of Top-5, leaving the computational benefit claim qualitative (precision).4) Suggestions for Improvement
   - Bolded titles with sub-point examples (matched one-to-one with Weaknesses and sub-point counts)- Broaden evaluation and quantify practical significance
     - Evaluate on at least one additional open-domain QA dataset and/or with another model or retriever (e.g., a different dense retriever or Top-10 setting) to test external validity (anchor new results to a table analogous to Table 1).
     - Report confidence intervals or standard deviations for CCV across different random permutation sets and seeds, matching the ± reporting shown once for the K=5 baseline (Table 1), to contextualize the +0.19 SubEM gain (statistical rigor).
     - Extend K-scaling beyond K=20 or include compute-normalized comparisons (e.g., accuracy per generated token) to assess whether the small gains justify added cost (augment Figure 2/Table 2 with such analyses).- Add missing methodological details to ensure reproducibility
     - Include the exact JSON prompt templates, output schemas, and any parsing/validation rules (augment Method, Section 3.2 with an appendix containing prompts).
     - Specify evidence-filtering implementation details: normalization steps, case/punctuation handling, tokenization for SubEM/F1, and exact matching thresholds (augment Method, Sections 3.2 and 3.4 with algorithmic pseudocode).
     - Detail permutation sampling: ensure uniqueness per query, report seeds, and describe run aggregation across permutation sets; add uncertainty estimates for CCV analogous to those shown for one baseline row (Table 1), to support significance assessments.- Preserve evidence grounding while maintaining coverage
     - Replace the binary “exact-quote vs. no-quote” options with a fuzzy alignment scheme (e.g., character-level edit distance or token-overlap threshold) and report citation accuracy under this scheme (augment Experiments, Section 4.4 with a new table parallel to Table 3).
     - Measure and report the rate of true supporting citations (manual or automated alignment) and its relationship to accuracy, especially under the relaxed setting (augment Section 4.5 with a citation-accuracy analysis).
     - Where the relaxed setting is used for performance, update Figure 1 and Method text to reflect the actual validation logic and add safeguards (e.g., minimum overlap) to deter spurious or adversarial citations (consistency between method and practice).- Strengthen baselines and clarify comparison framing
     - Separate permutation-based majority voting from multi-agent MoA in terminology and, if relevant, add a pure “Permutation Majority Vote” baseline label (revise Table 1 and Related Work, Section 2.3, for clarity).
     - Add inference-time baselines that leverage available signals: e.g., answer-frequency weighted by retrieval score, or simple citation-frequency voting without ID mapping, to isolate the effect of permutation-invariant ID aggregation (new ablation table next to Table 2).
     - For cross-prompt comparisons (Standard vs. JSON-citation), either unify the format or clearly segregate conclusions to avoid confounding; consider reporting both prompts for all methods where applicable (revise Section 4.2 narrative and tables).- Deepen diagnostic and ablation analyses
     - Report effect-size metrics (e.g., ROC-AUC for using citation-consistency as a predictor of correctness, or Cohen’s d for the agreement-score distributions) in addition to p-values (extend Section 4.5 with a new figure).
     - Provide qualitative case studies illustrating when CCV overrules majority voting beneficially and when it fails, including cited passages and answers (add a qualitative appendix).
     - Conduct sensitivity analyses for retrieval depth (Top-5 vs. Top-10) and decoding (temperature > 0 for diversity), adding plots analogous to Figure 2 to map operating regimes (extend Sections 4.1 and 4.3).- Improve clarity and presentation polish
     - Remove repeated “WARNING” lines and stray tokens (e.g., “ANALEMMA”), and ensure consistent section/caption formatting (Abstract; Method figure block; Experiments block).
     - Standardize nomenclature for baselines to avoid conflating multi-agent MoA with permutation ensembles (Table 1; Related Work), and explicitly define each baseline in Section 4.1.
     - Quantify the practical benefit (or lack thereof) of KV-cache sharing under random permutations and describe when shared prefixes occur; if minimal, clarify that compute benefits are mostly from batching (expand Method, Section 3.4 with measured throughput/latency).5) Score
  - Overall (10): 6 — A simple, training-free idea with clear formalization (Method, Equations (1)–(2); Figure 1) shows small but consistent gains on NQ (Table 1; Table 2; Figure 2) and a meaningful diagnostic (Section 4.5), though scope and effect sizes are limited.
  - Novelty (10): 6 — Aggregation by permutation-invariant citation IDs rather than answer frequency is a neat twist (Method, Section 3.3; Figure 1), positioned against self-consistency/ensembling (Related Work, Sections 2.3–2.4).
  - Technical Quality (10): 5 — Method is soundly specified (Equations (1)–(2)) and supported by diagnostics (Section 4.5), but evaluation breadth and statistical rigor are limited (Table 1 only one ± row; single dataset/model), and the relaxed evidence check risks spurious citations (Method, Section 3.4; Table 3).
  - Clarity (10): 6 — Core pipeline is clear (Figure 1; Method, Sections 3.1–3.3), but missing prompt/filtering details (Method, Section 3.2) and presentation artifacts (Abstract; “ANALEMMA”) detract from polish.
  - Confidence (5): 3 — Confidence is moderate; conclusions rely on a single dataset/model (Section 4.1) with small gains (Table 1; Table 2), and some implementation details are unspecified, limiting verification.
