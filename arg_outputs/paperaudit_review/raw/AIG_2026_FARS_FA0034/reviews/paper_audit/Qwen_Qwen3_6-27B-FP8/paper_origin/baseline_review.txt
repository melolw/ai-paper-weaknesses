1) Summary
The manuscript addresses the efficiency-quality tradeoff in two-stage video-text retrieval by proposing VidVec-RouteK, a training-free adaptive reranking budget allocation method. Rather than applying a fixed reranking budget $K$, the authors introduce cross-layer ranking disagreement—quantified via Jaccard distance between top-$k$ candidate sets across transformer layers—as a per-query confidence signal. A 3-tier routing architecture maps this disagreement score to budgets $K \in \{10, 60, 100\}$, dynamically allocating more compute to ambiguous queries. Evaluated on MSR-VTT and DiDeMo using VideoLLaMA3-7B, the approach achieves +0.9 and +1.5 R@1 improvements over margin-based routing while reducing average reranking forward passes by approximately 70%. The method requires no additional training and leverages intermediate layer representations to guide inference efficiency.2) Strengths
- **Practical Efficiency-Quality Tradeoff Resolution**
  - Table 1 and Section 4.2 demonstrate that the method reduces average reranking passes from 100 to ~30 while maintaining or improving R@1, which matters because it directly addresses the dominant computational bottleneck in deployment-ready retrieval pipelines.
  - Section 3.5 and the Abstract emphasize a training-free design that avoids auxiliary network training or fine-tuning, which matters because it lowers the adoption barrier and ensures seamless compatibility with existing pretrained multimodal large language models.
  - Table 1 and Section 4.2 show that 3-tier routing outperforms binary routing by +0.4 to +0.9 R@1, which matters because it empirically validates that nuanced budget allocation captures intermediate query difficulty more effectively than coarse binary splits.- **Novel and Principled Confidence Signal**
  - Equation 1 and Section 3.3 define cross-layer Jaccard disagreement as the routing metric rather than relying on simple similarity margins, which matters because it captures representation instability across network depth, offering a more robust estimate of model uncertainty.
  - Section 3.2 and Section 2 justify the selection of intermediate layers (20, 24, 27) based on prior findings that mid-to-late layers encode strong retrieval signals, which matters because it grounds the method in established MLLM representation behavior rather than arbitrary layer choices.
  - Table 2 and Section 4.3 present ablations showing robustness to alternative layer sets and ranking metrics (Kendall τ), which matters because it confirms the signal is not overly sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, enhancing reproducibility and practical reliability.- **Rigorous Experimental Validation**
  - Section 4.4 and Figure 1 report consistent query distributions across both benchmarks (~63% easy, 32% medium, 5% hard), which matters because it suggests the disagreement signal generalizes well beyond dataset-specific biases or domain shifts.
  - Table 1 and Section 4.2 highlight that selective reranking surpasses fixed $K=100$ on MSR-VTT despite 70% fewer passes, which matters because it provides empirical evidence that avoiding irrelevant deep candidates mitigates ranking noise rather than merely trading accuracy for speed.
  - Table 2 and Section 4.3 include a union pool variant analysis demonstrating that cross-layer diversity improves candidate coverage, which matters because it offers actionable insights for future candidate aggregation strategies beyond simple routing decisions.3) Weaknesses
- **Limited Generalization and Evaluation Scope**
  - Section 4.1 and Table 1 restrict evaluation to only two datasets (MSR-VTT, DiDeMo) and a single model (VideoLLaMA3-7B), which matters because it raises concerns about scalability to larger, more diverse video corpora or different MLLM architectures with varying depth and capacity.
  - Section 4.1 and the Conclusion omit evaluation on image-text or audio-text retrieval, which matters because it limits claims about the method's broader applicability to multimodal retrieval beyond video-specific temporal dynamics.
  - Section 3.4 and Section 4.1 calibrate thresholds $\tau_1, \tau_2$ on a validation set to target avg-K $\approx$ 30, which matters because it suggests potential overfitting to a specific compute budget rather than learning intrinsic, dataset-agnostic query difficulty boundaries.- **Incomplete Efficiency and Latency Analysis**
  - Section 3.5 and Table 1 measure efficiency solely via average forward passes (avg-K) without reporting wall-clock latency or throughput, which matters because forward pass count does not fully capture real-world inference costs, especially when accounting for memory access patterns and kernel launch overhead.
  - Section 3.5 claims the overhead of multi-layer extraction and Jaccard computation is negligible but provides no quantitative breakdown, which matters because unquantified routing overhead may significantly impact the net speedup in latency-sensitive or edge-deployed systems.
  - Section 3.5 lacks analysis of hardware-specific bottlenecks such as VRAM utilization for storing intermediate hidden states, which matters because practical deployment requires understanding how multi-layer readouts interact with GPU memory bandwidth and caching mechanisms.- **Weak Theoretical and Failure Mode Analysis**
  - Section 3.3 and Equation 1 present the disagreement signal without formal justification linking it to reranking correction probability, which matters because the correlation is empirically observed but lacks theoretical grounding in information theory, ranking stability, or uncertainty quantification.
  - Section 4.4 provides no failure case analysis or query-type breakdown (e.g., short vs. long queries, ambiguous vs. specific text), which matters because it limits understanding of when the routing signal breaks down or systematically misclassifies query difficulty.
  - Section 2 and Section 4.1 compare only against margin routing without benchmarking against other adaptive inference methods like early exiting or confidence-based pruning, which matters because it weakens the claim of superiority over the broader adaptive computation literature.4) Suggestions for Improvement
- **Expand Evaluation Scope and Generalization Testing**
  - Evaluate on additional video-text benchmarks (e.g., ActivityNet Captions, YouCook2) and at least one alternative MLLM architecture to verify cross-model robustness and validate claims beyond the current narrow experimental setting.
  - Test the method on image-text retrieval datasets (e.g., COCO, Flickr30K) to assess whether cross-layer disagreement generalizes to static visual modalities and confirm the signal's modality-agnostic utility.
  - Replace fixed avg-K calibration with a dynamic thresholding strategy or report performance across a continuous range of target budgets to demonstrate adaptability without reliance on validation-set tuning.- **Provide Comprehensive Efficiency and Latency Metrics**
  - Report end-to-end inference latency (ms/query) and throughput (queries/sec) on standard hardware, explicitly separating embedding extraction, routing computation, and reranking phases to reflect real-world deployment conditions.
  - Quantify the exact computational overhead of multi-layer forward hooks and Jaccard computation, providing a net speedup ratio that accounts for all pipeline stages rather than relying solely on avg-K reduction.
  - Include a memory footprint analysis for storing intermediate hidden states across layers, discussing trade-offs between compute savings and VRAM utilization to guide hardware-aware implementation.- **Strengthen Theoretical Grounding and Failure Analysis**
  - Provide a theoretical or empirical analysis linking disagreement magnitude to the probability of top-1 ranking correction, possibly using calibration curves, expected improvement metrics, or uncertainty quantification frameworks.
  - Conduct a qualitative and quantitative failure analysis, categorizing misrouted queries by text length, visual complexity, or domain specificity to identify systematic limitations of the disagreement signal.
  - Compare against additional adaptive inference baselines (e.g., confidence-based early exit, entropy-based routing, or patience-based criteria) to properly position the method within the broader adaptive computation landscape.5) Score
- Overall (10): 7 — The method offers a practical, training-free routing strategy with solid empirical gains, but limited evaluation scope and incomplete latency analysis constrain its broader impact.
- Novelty (10): 7 — Cross-layer Jaccard disagreement is a clever adaptation of layer-wise representation insights to retrieval routing, though it builds directly on established adaptive inference paradigms.
- Technical Quality (10): 6 — The implementation is sound and ablations are thorough, yet the reliance on validation-set threshold calibration and lack of theoretical grounding reduce technical rigor.
- Clarity (10): 8 — The manuscript is well-structured with clear equations, figures, and tables, making the method and results easy to follow.
- Confidence (5): 4 — High confidence based on clear experimental setup and reproducible training-free design, though limited dataset/model coverage slightly reduces certainty about generalization.
