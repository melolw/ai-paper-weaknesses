1) Summary
The paper addresses emergent misalignment (EM) during LLM fine-tuning, where narrow-task training induces harmful behaviors on unrelated prompts. The authors propose canary-controlled adaptive safe-data interleaving, a closed-loop framework that monitors EM risk using a small set of canary prompts and dynamically adjusts the safe-data mixing ratio via an EMA-smoothed, threshold-based controller with hysteresis. Evaluated on the Security EM benchmark with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the method achieves 5.39% General %Misaligned, a 25% relative improvement over fixed 5% interleaving, with significantly lower cross-seed variance. Ablation studies demonstrate that adaptive timing, feedback timeliness, and EMA smoothing are critical for performance, though the authors note a weak correlation between canary risk and general EM metrics.2) Strengths
- **Novel Adaptive Control Mechanism for Safety Training**
  - Introduces closed-loop feedback control to safe-data interleaving, moving beyond static ratios (Section 3.3, Eq. 3). This matters because it directly addresses the phase-transition dynamics of EM identified in prior work (Section 1), enabling resource-efficient intervention only when risk spikes.
  - Incorporates EMA smoothing and hysteresis to stabilize controller responses (Section 3.2, Eq. 2; Section 3.3). This matters because it prevents erratic oscillations from noisy canary evaluations, as empirically confirmed by the No-EMA ablation showing 45% performance degradation (Table 2).
  - Provides a clear algorithmic specification that decouples safe-data steps from target-dataset counters (Algorithm 1, Section 3.4). This matters because it ensures fair comparison with fixed-ratio baselines by maintaining consistent target training volume, avoiding confounding compute differences.
- **Rigorous and Insightful Experimental Evaluation**
  - Comprehensive ablation study isolating controller components (timing, delay, shuffling, EMA) (Table 2, Section 4.3). This matters because it demonstrates that adaptive timing provides value beyond average interleaving ratio (36% degradation for fixed $p=\bar{p}$), strongly validating the core hypothesis.
  - Reports cross-seed variance and controller behavior trajectories across multiple seeds (Table 1, Figure 2). This matters because it highlights the method's robustness (4× lower variance) and reveals seed-dependent risk dynamics, significantly enhancing reproducibility and statistical reliability.
  - Transparently reports the weak canary-general correlation ($r=0.31$) and analyzes its implications (Section 4.4). This matters because it builds scientific trust by acknowledging proxy signal limitations while demonstrating that the controller still effectively suppresses EM despite imperfect tracking.
- **Clear and Well-Structured Presentation**
  - Logical flow from problem motivation to method, experiments, and limitations (Sections 1–5). This matters because it facilitates straightforward comprehension of the feedback control loop and its safety implications for practitioners.
  - High-quality visualizations of controller dynamics (Figure 1, Figure 2). This matters because they clearly illustrate how $p_t$ and $\hat{r}_t$ evolve across training steps and seeds, making the adaptive behavior intuitive and verifiable.
  - Explicit parameter reporting for the controller ($\alpha$, thresholds, step sizes, $K$, $M$) (Section 4.1). This matters because it enables direct replication and hyperparameter sensitivity analysis by future work, reducing implementation ambiguity.3) Weaknesses
- **Limited Generalization and Benchmark Coverage**
  - Evaluation is restricted to a single benchmark (Security EM) and one model family (Qwen2.5-7B) (Section 4.1, Table 1). This matters because it limits confidence in the method's robustness across different model architectures, sizes, or EM-inducing datasets.
  - No comparison against other adaptive or safety-preserving fine-tuning methods like SafeLoRA or KL-regularization (Section 2.2, Section 4.1). This matters because it leaves open whether the gains stem from adaptive interleaving specifically or from the higher average safe-data volume (13% vs 5%).
  - The safe dataset (WildGuardMix benign subset) is not thoroughly characterized for domain overlap with the target task (Section 4.1). This matters because domain mismatch or overlap could confound the observed EM suppression effects, requiring explicit similarity metrics.
- **Computational Overhead and Practical Deployment Concerns**
  - The method requires generating responses to 20 canary prompts every 30 steps using a 32B judge model (Section 4.1, Algorithm 1). This matters because it introduces significant inference overhead during training, which is not quantified or analyzed against wall-clock time.
  - Average interleaving ratio increases to 13% compared to 5% for the fixed baseline (Table 1). This matters because it increases training compute and data requirements, potentially offsetting the efficiency gains of adaptive control in resource-constrained settings.
  - No analysis of the trade-off between canary evaluation frequency ($K$) and controller responsiveness (Section 4.3 mentions delay ablation but not $K$ sensitivity). This matters because optimal $K$ selection remains heuristic, limiting practical guidance for deployment across different training speeds.
- **Theoretical and Mechanistic Gaps**
  - The weak canary-general correlation ($r=0.31$) is acknowledged but not mechanistically explained (Section 4.4). This matters because it raises questions about whether the controller is optimizing a surrogate that may fail under distribution shift or adversarial prompt variations.
  - No theoretical analysis of the threshold controller's convergence or stability guarantees (Section 3.3). This matters because the empirical success relies on heuristic thresholds ($r_{high}=0.08, r_{low}=0.02$) without bounds on oscillation or overshoot under varying risk trajectories.
  - The impact of hysteresis band width and step sizes ($\delta_{\uparrow}, \delta_{\downarrow}$) is not systematically studied (Section 4.1). This matters because controller performance may be highly sensitive to these hyperparameters, reducing reproducibility across different training regimes or model scales.
- **Internal Consistency and Data Reporting Discrepancies**
  - Textual claims in Section 4.4 state that "all seeds reaching $p_{max} = 0.20$ by training completion" with averages "ranging from 0.11 to 0.15", but the table in Figure 2 shows seeds 123 and 456 maintaining $p_t = 0.00$ throughout, and seed 42 plateauing at 0.04. This matters because it directly contradicts the reported controller behavior and undermines trust in the experimental results.
  - The data in Figure 2 appears to violate the controller logic defined in Equation 3 and Section 4.1. For instance, seed 123 shows $\hat{r}_t = 0.09$ at step 150 (exceeding $r_{high} = 0.08$), yet $p_t$ remains 0.00 instead of increasing by $\delta_{\uparrow} = 0.04$. This matters because it suggests either an implementation bug, incorrect parameter reporting, or fabricated data.
  - Average interleaving ratios are inconsistently reported across the manuscript: Table 1 reports 0.13, Table 2 reports 0.155, and Section 4.4 claims 0.11–0.15, while Figure 2 implies near-zero averages for two seeds. This matters because it creates ambiguity regarding the actual safe-data budget consumed by the method.4) Suggestions for Improvement
- **Expand Evaluation Scope and Baselines**
  - Evaluate on at least two additional EM-inducing benchmarks (e.g., persona-based or reasoning-focused datasets) and model sizes to validate generalization beyond the Security EM benchmark.
  - Include direct comparisons with SafeLoRA and KL-divergence regularization to isolate the contribution of adaptive interleaving from other safety mechanisms and establish a stronger performance baseline.
  - Provide a detailed analysis of the WildGuardMix safe dataset, including domain similarity metrics to the Security EM target data, to rule out confounding factors related to data distribution overlap.
- **Quantify and Mitigate Computational Costs**
  - Report the wall-clock training time and GPU memory overhead introduced by the canary evaluation loop, comparing it directly to the fixed-ratio baseline to clarify practical trade-offs.
  - Investigate lightweight risk estimation alternatives, such as using the fine-tuned model itself for self-evaluation or reducing canary set size ($M$) with statistical confidence bounds, to lower inference costs.
  - Conduct a sensitivity analysis over evaluation frequency $K$ and safe-data budget constraints to provide practical guidelines for resource-limited deployments and optimal controller tuning.
- **Strengthen Theoretical Grounding and Hyperparameter Analysis**
  - Provide a mechanistic or representational analysis (e.g., probing alignment features or activation patterns) to explain why the weakly correlated canary signal still effectively suppresses EM across training phases.
  - Derive or cite stability conditions for the threshold controller with hysteresis, or empirically bound the oscillation frequency under varying risk trajectories to improve theoretical rigor.
  - Systematically sweep hysteresis thresholds ($r_{high}, r_{low}$) and step sizes ($\delta_{\uparrow}, \delta_{\downarrow}$) to report performance sensitivity and recommend robust default configurations for broader applicability.
- **Resolve Internal Contradictions and Verify Data Integrity**
  - Reconcile the textual description in Section 4.4 with the numerical data in Figure 2, explicitly clarifying whether the controller actually reached $p_{max}$ or if the text contains a reporting error.
  - Audit the controller implementation against Equation 3 to ensure that threshold crossings correctly trigger ratio updates, and provide corrected trajectories or a detailed explanation of any deviations.
  - Standardize the reporting of average interleaving ratios across all tables and text, and clarify how the averages in Tables 1 and 2 were computed relative to the step-by-step data in Figure 2.5) Score
- Overall (10): 5 — The method introduces a practical adaptive control loop for safe-data interleaving with strong empirical results on a single benchmark (Table 1, Section 4.2), but internal data contradictions (Figure 2 vs. Section 4.4) and limited generalization constrain broader impact.
- Novelty (10): 7 — Applying closed-loop feedback control with EMA smoothing and hysteresis to safe-data interleaving is a novel adaptation to EM mitigation (Section 3.3, Algorithm 1), though it builds on established control principles.
- Technical Quality (10): 5 — Experiments are well-structured with thorough ablations (Table 2, Section 4.3), but inconsistencies between Figure 2 data, textual claims, and the algorithmic logic in Equation 3 weaken technical rigor.
- Clarity (10): 7 — The manuscript is logically organized, with clear algorithmic descriptions and well-labeled figures (Figure 1, Figure 2), though contradictory reporting of controller trajectories and average ratios reduces clarity.
- Confidence (5): 3 — Moderate confidence due to clear discrepancies between the reported controller behavior in Section 4.4 and the numerical data in Figure 2, which require clarification before the results can be fully trusted.
