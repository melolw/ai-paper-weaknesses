1) Summary
This paper investigates the counterintuitive degradation of task success when increasing verification budgets in sequential verify-and-retry loops for web agents. The authors identify *trajectory drift*—where false-negative verifications trigger agent re-browsing that yields inferior products—as the primary failure mode. To mitigate this, they propose *chunked budget allocation*, which concentrates a fixed verification budget into fewer sequential cycles with more parallel world-model rollouts per cycle. Evaluated on WebShop, allocating the entire budget to a single parallel cycle ($M=1$) yields a 25× improvement in success rate (21.50% vs. 0.86%) over sequential verification. The study further demonstrates that consensus aggregation provides no benefit due to severe model miscalibration, concluding that structural budget allocation outweighs aggregation sophistication when world models are poorly calibrated.2) Strengths
- **Clear Identification of a Novel Failure Mode**
  - Evidence: Section 4.4 and Table 2 quantify trajectory drift as accounting for 60.5% of failures when chunked verification succeeds but sequential fails. Why it matters: Provides a concrete, empirically grounded diagnostic for agentic systems, shifting focus from pure verification accuracy to interaction dynamics.
  - Evidence: Section 1 explicitly models the feedback loop between false negatives and re-browsing that compounds errors across cycles. Why it matters: Challenges standard test-time scaling assumptions and explains the non-monotonic regression phenomenon observed in prior work.
  - Evidence: Section 4.4 isolates episodes where sequential verification explicitly caused worse product selections compared to the original proposal. Why it matters: Demonstrates causal linkage between verification structure and downstream agent behavior, strengthening the paper's core thesis.- **Rigorous Experimental Design & Ablation**
  - Evidence: Section 4.3 and Figure 2 systematically vary cycle count $M \in \{1, 2, 5, 10\}$ while fixing total budget $B=10$. Why it matters: Isolates the structural effect of sequential cycles from budget magnitude, providing clean evidence for the proposed mechanism.
  - Evidence: Section 4.5 and Table 3 conduct a temperature ablation comparing stochastic (temp=0.7) and deterministic (temp=0.0) decoding. Why it matters: Rules out stochastic diversity as the source of improvement, confirming the benefit is architectural rather than sampling-based.
  - Evidence: Section 4.1 and Table 1 report 95% bootstrap confidence intervals across 3 seeds and 600 episodes per condition. Why it matters: Ensures statistical rigor and reproducibility, validating the claimed 25× improvement against random variance.- **Practical & Actionable Insights for Agentic Systems**
  - Evidence: Section 4.6 reveals that consensus aggregation degrades utility because $\hat{p}=0$ for 98.4% of cycles, with Brier score 0.28 and ECE 0.28. Why it matters: Warns practitioners against blindly applying self-consistency to poorly calibrated verifiers, saving compute and avoiding performance traps.
  - Evidence: Section 4.3 and Figure 2 show monotonic performance degradation as $M$ increases, with $M=1$ consistently optimal. Why it matters: Offers a simple, low-complexity deployment heuristic that requires no hyperparameter tuning or model retraining.
  - Evidence: Abstract footnote 1 and Section 5 explicitly acknowledge automated generation and open-source the implementation. Why it matters: Enhances transparency, facilitates community validation, and aligns with open-science best practices.3) Weaknesses
- **Limited Generalizability & Scope**
  - Evidence: Section 4.1 and Section 5 restrict evaluation to a single benchmark (WebShop) and a single world model (Qwen2.5-7B). Why it matters: Limits confidence in whether findings transfer to other irreversible-action domains (e.g., code execution, API calls) or better-calibrated architectures.
  - Evidence: Table 1 shows absolute success rates remain modest (max 21.50%), with no baseline reported for zero-verification conditions. Why it matters: Makes it difficult to disentangle verification benefits from inherent agent limitations or task difficulty.
  - Evidence: Section 2 discusses error recovery methods like WebRollback but does not include them as experimental baselines. Why it matters: Leaves open whether chunked allocation is superior to other established recovery strategies, weakening comparative impact.- **Incomplete Analysis of Threshold & Calibration Dynamics**
  - Evidence: Section 3.2 and Section 4.3 fix the consensus threshold $\tau$ at 0.1 or 0.5 without systematic tuning or justification. Why it matters: Threshold choice heavily impacts pass/fail rates, especially with miscalibrated models, and unoptimized thresholds may mask potential gains.
  - Evidence: Section 4.6 reports calibration metrics but does not explore recalibration techniques (e.g., temperature scaling, Platt scaling). Why it matters: Misses an opportunity to determine if fixing calibration would restore the utility of consensus aggregation, limiting the completeness of the analysis.
  - Evidence: Section 4.3 notes a non-monotonic pattern ($M=2$ worse than $M=5$) but attributes it vaguely to threshold interactions without deeper mechanistic breakdown. Why it matters: Leaves a key empirical observation partially unexplained, reducing confidence in the proposed structural narrative.- **Methodological Constraints & Assumptions**
  - Evidence: Section 4.1 fixes budget $B=10$ without analyzing cost-performance trade-offs across different budget scales. Why it matters: Real-world deployments face varying compute constraints; scalability and diminishing returns of the chunked approach remain untested.
  - Evidence: Section 3.2 treats parallel rollouts as fully independent without addressing context-window sharing or API rate limits. Why it matters: In practice, parallel LLM calls may share context or hit infrastructure limits, affecting real-world feasibility and latency.
  - Evidence: Section 3.3 defines Condition C (no-aggregation) by arbitrarily using the first rollout's prediction. Why it matters: Lacks theoretical justification and raises questions about whether other simple heuristics (e.g., random selection, max confidence) would yield identical results.4) Suggestions for Improvement
- **Expand Evaluation Scope & Baselines**
  - Evaluate on at least one additional domain with irreversible actions (e.g., code execution or API interaction) and a second world model architecture to test generalizability, directly addressing the single-domain limitation in Section 4.1 and Section 5.
  - Report baseline success rates without any verification to contextualize the 21.50% absolute performance, addressing the missing zero-verification baseline in Table 1.
  - Include a direct comparison with explicit rollback mechanisms (e.g., WebRollback) to position chunked allocation within the broader error-recovery literature, addressing the gap noted in Section 2.- **Deepen Threshold & Calibration Analysis**
  - Conduct a systematic sweep of $\tau \in [0.0, 1.0]$ for each $M$ configuration and report the optimal threshold alongside sensitivity analysis, addressing the fixed-threshold choice in Section 3.2 and Section 4.3.
  - Apply standard recalibration methods (e.g., isotonic regression or temperature scaling) to the world model's outputs and re-evaluate consensus aggregation to determine if calibration fixes restore its utility, addressing the unexplored recalibration space in Section 4.6.
  - Provide a more detailed mechanistic breakdown of the $M=2$ vs. $M=5$ anomaly by logging agent re-browsing depth and product quality metrics per cycle, addressing the incomplete explanation in Section 4.3.- **Strengthen Methodological Rigor & Practicality**
  - Test the chunked allocation strategy across a range of budgets ($B \in \{5, 10, 20, 50\}$) to demonstrate scalability and identify potential diminishing returns, addressing the fixed-budget constraint in Section 4.1.
  - Discuss practical constraints of parallel rollouts, such as context-window sharing, API rate limits, and latency, and propose mitigation strategies for real-world deployment, addressing the independence assumption in Section 3.2.
  - Replace the arbitrary "first-rollout" heuristic in Condition C with a principled alternative (e.g., random selection or confidence-weighted sampling) and verify that results remain robust, addressing the ungrounded design choice in Section 3.3.5) Score
- Overall (10): 6 — The paper presents a compelling structural insight into verification budget allocation (Section 4.3, Figure 2) and identifies a novel failure mode (Section 4.4), but limited evaluation scope and unaddressed calibration dynamics constrain its broader impact.
- Novelty (10): 7 — Identifying trajectory drift as the cause of non-monotonic regression (Section 1, Table 2) and demonstrating that allocation structure outweighs aggregation sophistication (Section 4.6) offer fresh perspectives on test-time scaling for agents.
- Technical Quality (10): 6 — The experimental design is rigorous with proper ablations and confidence intervals (Section 4.1, Table 3), yet the fixed threshold choice, lack of recalibration exploration, and single-domain evaluation weaken technical completeness.
- Clarity (10): 8 — The manuscript is well-organized, with clear problem formulation (Section 3.1), intuitive figures (Figure 1, Figure 2), and concise explanations of mechanisms (Section 4.4, Section 4.6).
- Confidence (5): 4 — High confidence in the structural findings and ablation results (Section 4.3, Section 4.5), though limited by the single benchmark and model evaluation scope (Section 4.1, Section 5).
