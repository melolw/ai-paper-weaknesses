1) Summary
The authors introduce LiveMedBench-Ask1, a controlled evaluation protocol designed to assess whether allowing medical LLMs to ask a single clarifying question improves the quality of their advice. Using a subset of 657 cases from LiveMedBench where exactly one critical patient information slot is masked, the study evaluates GPT-4.1 and Qwen3-14B under three conditions: a masked baseline (A), an Ask1 protocol with a deterministic slot oracle (B), and an unmasked upper bound (C). The results demonstrate that while models identify missing information at rates significantly above chance, this capability does not translate to improved rubric scores. The authors attribute this negative result to minimal information headroom—where the performance gap between masked and unmasked conditions is negligible—and to a failure in effectively integrating the retrieved information into the final response (Abstract, Section 4.2).2) Strengths
*   **Rigorous Controlled Experimental Design**
    *   The study isolates the effect of a single clarifying question by using a deterministic slot oracle, eliminating confounds from noisy agent interactions common in multi-turn benchmarks (Section 3.3, Figure 1). This matters for technical soundness, as it cleanly separates question-generation capability from downstream answer quality.
    *   The three-condition design (Masked, Ask1, Unmasked) allows for precise measurement of both the "information headroom" (C-A gap) and the actual improvement gained from asking (B-A gap) (Section 3.2, Table 2). This matters for experimental rigor, enabling a causal interpretation of whether clarification adds marginal utility.
    *   The use of a contamination-free benchmark (LiveMedBench) ensures that the evaluation reflects genuine reasoning capabilities rather than memorization (Section 1, Section 2). This matters for impact, as it addresses a critical validity concern in modern LLM medical evaluations.
*   **Comprehensive Statistical Analysis**
    *   The authors provide bootstrap confidence intervals for score differences, correctly identifying that the B-A improvement is not statistically significant for either model (Table 2, Section 4.2). This matters for technical soundness, preventing overinterpretation of negligible score fluctuations.
    *   Binomial tests are used to validate that slot hit rates are significantly above the chance baseline of 12.5% (1/8 slot types) (Section 4.2). This matters for experimental rigor, confirming that models are not randomly guessing but actively targeting missing slots.
    *   A Welch t-test is employed to analyze the relationship between slot hits and score improvements, providing statistical backing for the claim that hitting the slot does not guarantee better scores (Section 4.4). This matters for clarity, as it quantitatively supports the "integration bottleneck" hypothesis.
*   **Insightful Interpretation of Negative Results**
    *   The paper identifies "minimal information headroom" as a fundamental limitation of single-slot masking, offering a valuable diagnostic for future benchmark design (Section 4.2, Figure 2). This matters for impact, guiding researchers toward multi-slot or multi-turn protocols where interactive gains are more plausible.
    *   The analysis distinguishes between question generation capability and answer integration, revealing that models struggle to leverage obtained information even when they ask the right question (Section 4.4, Section 5). This matters for novelty, shifting the research focus from prompting strategies to reasoning pipeline limitations.
    *   Per-slot-type analysis highlights clinical nuances, such as the higher potential for improvement in "pregnancy_status" cases, suggesting domain-specific sensitivity (Figure 2, Section 4.3). This matters for clarity, providing actionable insights for targeted clinical AI applications.3) Weaknesses
*   **Limited and Skewed Slot Distribution**
    *   The subset is dominated by "current_medications" (43.1%), while critical slots like "anticoagulation" have very few samples (n=8), limiting the generalizability of findings across slot types (Table 1). This matters for experimental rigor, as aggregate metrics may be disproportionately driven by a single clinical category.
    *   The selection of only eight slot types may not capture the full complexity of clinical information gaps, such as social determinants of health or family history (Section 5, Limitations). This matters for impact, restricting the protocol's applicability to broader real-world consultation scenarios.
    *   The skewed distribution means that aggregate results are heavily influenced by the medication slot, potentially masking trends in other categories (Table 1, Section 4.3). This matters for technical soundness, as it complicates the interpretation of overall model capabilities.
*   **Brittle Oracle Matching Mechanism**
    *   The oracle relies on case-insensitive string matching against predefined slot types, which is fragile and may fail to recognize semantically equivalent questions (e.g., "Is the patient pregnant?" vs. "pregnancy_status") (Section 3.3). This matters for technical soundness, as it introduces a measurement error that could artificially depress slot hit rates.
    *   The binary response ("ground-truth value" or "unavailable") does not account for partial matches or nuanced inquiries, potentially penalizing models that ask valid but slightly differently phrased questions (Section 3.3). This matters for experimental rigor, limiting the ecological validity of the oracle compared to real clinical information retrieval.
    *   There is no discussion of how the oracle handles questions that are relevant but do not match the specific masked slot, which could lead to false negatives in slot hit rates (Section 3.3). This matters for clarity, as it leaves ambiguity in how model inquiry strategies are evaluated.
*   **Data Inconsistency and Evaluation Sensitivity**
    *   There is a direct numerical contradiction between Figure 2(b) and Figure 2(d) regarding the "Renal" slot type for Qwen3-14B: Figure 2(b) reports a B-A improvement of -1.8, whereas Figure 2(d) lists it as 0.2 for the identical C-A gap of 0.2, undermining data reliability (Figure 2). This matters for technical soundness, as unexplained discrepancies in supplementary tables cast doubt on the reproducibility of the reported results.
    *   The rubric scores are consistently low (around 0.36), and the C-A gap is minimal (0.6–0.9 percentage points), raising concerns that the rubric may not be sensitive enough to detect improvements from single-slot information (Table 2, Section 4.2). This matters for experimental rigor, suggesting the evaluation metric may be ceiling/floor-bounded or misaligned with the intervention.
    *   The study uses a single LLM grader (GPT-4.1) without reporting inter-grader reliability or human validation, which is critical for rubric-based evaluations (Section 3.4, Section 5). This matters for impact, as LLM-as-judge bias remains an open concern in medical AI assessment.4) Suggestions for Improvement
*   **Expand and Balance Slot Diversity**
    *   Increase the number of slot types to include a broader range of clinical information (e.g., social history, family history) to better reflect real-world consultation gaps.
    *   Oversample rare slot types or stratify the analysis to ensure that findings are not dominated by the "current_medications" category.
    *   Report results separately for high-frequency and low-frequency slots to assess if the "minimal headroom" finding holds across different clinical contexts.
*   **Enhance Oracle Robustness**
    *   Replace or augment string matching with semantic similarity measures (e.g., embeddings) to better capture the intent of clarifying questions.
    *   Allow for partial matches or synonyms in the oracle logic to reduce false negatives in slot hit rates.
    *   Provide more informative feedback for non-matching questions to help models refine their inquiry strategy in future iterations.
*   **Resolve Data Discrepancies and Improve Evaluation Transparency**
    *   Reconcile the numerical mismatch between Figure 2(b) and Figure 2(d) for the Qwen3-14B Renal slot to ensure internal consistency across all reported tables and plots.
    *   Analyze rubric criteria individually to identify which specific aspects of the response are affected by the masked information, providing deeper insight into the integration bottleneck.
    *   Include a human evaluation subset to validate the LLM-as-judge scores and assess inter-rater reliability.5) Score
- Overall (10): 6 — The paper presents a well-controlled study with insightful negative results, but the limited slot diversity, brittle oracle mechanism, and internal data inconsistencies constrain its impact.
- Novelty (10): 7 — The Ask1 protocol is a novel contribution for isolating single-turn clarification, though it builds on existing benchmarks.
- Technical Quality (10): 6 — The statistical analysis is rigorous, but the oracle design, rubric sensitivity, and unexplained numerical discrepancies in Figure 2 present methodological limitations.
- Clarity (10): 8 — The manuscript is clearly written, with well-structured sections and effective use of figures and tables.
- Confidence (5): 4 — The reviewer is confident in the assessment based on the clear methodology, though the data inconsistency in Figure 2 slightly reduces certainty.
