1) Summary
The paper addresses a novel security vulnerability in structured-output LLM APIs known as "control-plane jailbreaks," where attackers embed harmful instructions within JSON schema `enum` or `const` values. Because constrained decoding forces the model to output these values verbatim, traditional safety alignments are bypassed. The authors propose Selective DeLex-JSON, a training-free defense that intercepts schemas, identifies suspicious literals using a conjunction-based heuristic (checking for length >20, whitespace, and imperative verbs), and replaces them with opaque placeholders. Experiments on HarmBench demonstrate that the method achieves 0% attack success rate on Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B against EnumAttack, while maintaining a low 1.1% benign schema modification rate, thereby Pareto-dominating the Reject-Only baseline.2) Strengths
*   **Novel Identification of Control-Plane Attack Vector**
    *   The paper correctly distinguishes between data-plane attacks (prompt injection) and control-plane attacks (schema manipulation), a distinction often overlooked in existing literature (Section 2.3, Section 3.1).
    *   It highlights that constrained decoding mechanisms, while ensuring syntactic validity, inadvertently create a forced-output channel for malicious payloads (Section 1, Section 2.2).
    *   This focus addresses a critical gap in LLM security, as standard input guards (like Llama Guard) fail to detect intent embedded in structural definitions (Table 1, Section 4.2).
*   **High Efficacy Against EnumAttack**
    *   The defense achieves a 0% Attack Success Rate (ASR) on HarmBench for both Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B, completely neutralizing the baseline EnumAttack which had a 22.0% ASR (Table 1, Section 4.2).
    *   The ablation study confirms that the specific removal of forced literals is the key factor, as the "Strip-Only" variant (removing metadata only) fails to reduce ASR (Table 3, Section 4.4).
    *   The method generalizes across different model architectures (Llama and Qwen), suggesting the defense is model-agnostic (Section 4.2).
*   **Minimal Impact on Benign Utility**
    *   The defense incurs only a 1.1% benign schema modification rate across 8,825 real-world schemas, indicating high precision in distinguishing attacks from legitimate use cases (Table 2, Section 4.3).
    *   Utility metrics show negligible degradation in JSON validity (-0.38pp) and schema compliance (-0.70pp), well within acceptable production thresholds (Table 2, Section 4.3).
    *   The approach Pareto-dominates the "Reject-Only" baseline by reducing the benign cost from 4.4% to 1.1% while maintaining identical safety guarantees (Figure 2, Section 4.5).
*   **Practical Deployability**
    *   The method is training-free and operates as a preprocessing step, requiring no modification to the underlying LLM or inference engine (Abstract, Section 3.2).
    *   It integrates seamlessly with existing constrained decoding frameworks like vLLM and XGrammar (Section 3.4).
    *   The use of opaque placeholders preserves schema structure, allowing the constrained decoding engine to function without error (Section 3.4).3) Weaknesses
*   **Fragility to Chunked-Payload Attacks**
    *   The defense relies heavily on a length threshold (>20 characters), making it vulnerable to attacks that split harmful instructions into short chunks (Section 4.6, Figure 3).
    *   The paper explicitly demonstrates that chunked attacks evade the suspicion function entirely (0% flagged chunks), resulting in residual ASR of 8–14% (Figure 3, Section 4.6).
    *   This limitation suggests the heuristic is easily bypassed by simple obfuscation techniques, reducing the robustness of the defense in adversarial settings (Section 4.6).
*   **Lack of Semantic Understanding in Suspicion Function**
    *   The suspicion function is purely syntactic, relying on length, whitespace, and keyword matching (imperative verbs) without understanding the semantic meaning of the literal (Section 3.3).
    *   This approach may fail to detect harmful content that does not use imperative verbs or is phrased as a question or statement (Section 3.3).
    *   Conversely, it may flag benign long strings that happen to contain whitespace or verbs but are not malicious (Section 3.3, though the 1.1% false positive rate suggests this is currently low, it is a theoretical risk).
*   **Insufficient Ablation on Heuristic Thresholds**
    *   The paper sets the length threshold at 20 characters but does not provide an ablation study justifying this specific value (Section 3.3).
    *   It is unclear how sensitive the benign modification rate and ASR are to variations in this threshold (e.g., 15 vs. 25 characters) (Section 3.3).
    *   Without this analysis, the choice of threshold appears arbitrary, and the optimal trade-off point is not rigorously established (Section 3.3).
*   **Ambiguity in Downstream Placeholder Resolution**
    *   While the paper mentions a mapping table to restore original values, it does not detail how this integration works in a real-world API pipeline (Section 3.4).
    *   It is unclear how downstream consumers handle the `OPTION_0` placeholders if the mapping is not applied, potentially breaking application logic (Section 3.4).
    *   The separation of safety evaluation (excluding forced literals) from utility evaluation raises questions about how the restored content is validated for safety post-generation (Section 3.4).4) Suggestions for Improvement
*   **Enhance Detection with Semantic Analysis**
    *   Incorporate a lightweight semantic classifier or embedding-based similarity check to detect malicious intent in short strings, addressing the chunked-attack vulnerability (Section 4.6).
    *   Evaluate the performance of the defense when augmented with a small LLM or embedding model to assess the trade-off between computational cost and robustness (Section 3.3).
    *   Consider analyzing the context of the literal within the schema (e.g., field name, description) to improve detection accuracy (Section 3.3).
*   **Conduct Threshold Sensitivity Analysis**
    *   Provide an ablation study varying the length threshold (e.g., 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 characters) to demonstrate the impact on both ASR and benign modification rate (Section 3.3).
    *   Discuss the rationale for choosing 20 characters and whether adaptive thresholds based on schema complexity could improve performance (Section 3.3).
    *   Analyze the distribution of literal lengths in benign schemas to justify the threshold choice statistically (Section 4.3).
*   **Clarify Downstream Integration and Safety Validation**
    *   Provide a concrete example or protocol for how the mapping table is used to restore original values and how downstream systems handle the placeholders (Section 3.4).
    *   Discuss the safety implications of restoring the original literals post-generation, ensuring that the restored content does not reintroduce harm (Section 3.4).
    *   Elaborate on how the defense interacts with the constrained decoding engine when placeholders are present, ensuring no edge cases cause generation errors (Section 3.4).
*   **Expand Evaluation Scope**
    *   Test the defense against a broader range of attack variants beyond EnumAttack and chunked payloads, such as attacks using `description` fields or nested schema structures (Section 4.1).
    *   Evaluate the defense on a larger and more diverse set of models to further confirm generalizability (Section 4.1).
    *   Include a human evaluation of the benign schemas flagged by the defense to assess the practical impact of the 1.1% modification rate (Section 4.3).5) Score

*   Novelty (10): 7 — The focus on control-plane jailbreaks via JSON schemas is a distinct and timely contribution to LLM security, differentiating it from standard prompt injection defenses.
*   Technical Quality (10): 6 — The method is technically sound and well-evaluated, but the heuristic-based approach is easily bypassed (as shown in the paper), and the lack of threshold ablation weakens the technical rigor.
