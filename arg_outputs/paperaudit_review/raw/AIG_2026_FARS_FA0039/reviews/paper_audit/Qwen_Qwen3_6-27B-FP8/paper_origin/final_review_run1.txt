1) Summary
The paper addresses gradient quality degradation in distributed reinforcement learning for LLMs caused by policy staleness. Standard GRPO applies per-token importance ratios that ignore sequential dependencies, potentially over-weighting tokens following flawed reasoning prefixes. The authors propose Prefix-Ratio GRPO, which modifies the importance ratio to $\tilde{\rho}_t = \underline{\rho}_t \cdot \rho_t$, where $\underline{\rho}_t = \min_{k<t} \rho_k$ captures the lowest token ratio in the preceding prefix. This formulation selectively dampens gradients for tokens following unlikely prefixes while preserving gradients for reliable sequences. Evaluated on AIME24 with Qwen3-8B at staleness $S=11$, the method achieves 0.500 avg@64, outperforming vanilla and tight-clip GRPO by 10 and 13.3 percentage points respectively, with selectivity analysis demonstrating targeted gradient filtering.2) Strengths
- **Clear Motivation Aligned with Autoregressive Dependencies**
  - Evidence: Section 3.2 explicitly identifies the mismatch between independent per-token ratios and sequential chain-of-thought generation. Why it matters: This grounds the method in a well-known structural property of LLMs, enhancing technical soundness by targeting a specific failure mode of standard importance sampling.
  - Evidence: Figure 1 visually contrasts token-level independence with prefix-aware dependency propagation. Why it matters: The diagram clarifies the core mechanism, improving clarity and helping readers quickly grasp the sequential dampening intuition.
  - Evidence: Section 3.2 links low prefix probability to unreliable downstream reasoning steps. Why it matters: This provides a strong conceptual justification for why prefix information should modulate gradient updates, supporting the method's novelty in the GRPO context.
- **Empirical Validation with Mechanistic Analysis**
  - Evidence: Table 1 reports consistent performance gains (+10pp over vanilla GRPO) on AIME24. Why it matters: Demonstrates practical impact and experimental rigor by showing measurable downstream improvement under controlled staleness.
  - Evidence: Table 2 quantifies a 4.42× selectivity ratio, dampening 99.4% of bad-prefix tokens while preserving 77.4% of good-prefix tokens. Why it matters: Provides concrete evidence that the method operates through selective gradient filtering rather than uniform suppression, strengthening technical credibility.
  - Evidence: Figure 2 and Section 4.4 show nearly identical training reward curves across methods despite divergent final performance. Why it matters: Isolates the improvement mechanism to gradient quality rather than training stability, offering a nuanced empirical insight that enhances the paper's analytical depth.
- **Practical Implementation and System Compatibility**
  - Evidence: Section 3.4 states the modification requires only behavior-policy log-probabilities and runs in $O(T)$ time. Why it matters: Ensures low computational overhead, making the method highly deployable in existing RLVR pipelines without sacrificing efficiency.
  - Evidence: Section 3.4 notes compatibility with bounded-staleness systems like ECHO-2 and veRL. Why it matters: Broadens the method's applicability to real-world distributed training setups, increasing its practical impact and adoption potential.
  - Evidence: Section 3.4 emphasizes that the change is learner-side only, requiring no rollout infrastructure modifications. Why it matters: Reduces engineering friction and deployment risk, which is critical for large-scale LLM training systems.3) Weaknesses
- **Limited Theoretical Justification for the Minimum Operator**
  - Evidence: Equation 3 defines $\underline{\rho}_t = \min_{k<t} \rho_k$ without deriving why the minimum is theoretically preferable to the product $\prod_{k<t} \rho_k$ or other aggregations. Why it matters: Lacks rigorous grounding, reducing technical soundness and leaving open questions about gradient variance or bias introduced by the min operation.
  - Evidence: Section 2 cites MinPRO for prefix importance intuition but does not adapt its theoretical bounds to GRPO's clipped surrogate objective. Why it matters: The method's stability and convergence properties under clipping remain unproven, weakening the theoretical foundation.
  - Evidence: Section 3.3 presents the final objective (Equation 5) without analyzing how $\min_{k<t} \rho_k$ interacts with the PPO clipping bounds $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$. Why it matters: Without this analysis, it is unclear whether the prefix ratio could inadvertently cause gradient starvation or violate trust-region assumptions.
- **Ambiguous Definition of Prefix Quality and Dampening Thresholds**
  - Evidence: Table 2 and Section 4.3 report "good-prefix" vs "bad-prefix" dampening rates but do not specify the criterion used to classify prefixes. Why it matters: Without explicit definitions (e.g., based on final reward, token correctness, or a fixed $\rho$ threshold), the selectivity metrics cannot be independently verified, undermining experimental rigor.
  - Evidence: Section 4.3 defines a token as "dampened" if its effective importance ratio falls below a threshold, but the exact threshold value is omitted. Why it matters: The choice of threshold directly impacts the reported selectivity ratio; its absence prevents reproducibility and fair comparison with baselines.
  - Evidence: Section 3.2 assumes low prefix probability implies flawed reasoning, but does not address cases where early tokens are correct yet naturally low-probability under the current policy. Why it matters: Over-dampening valid but rare reasoning paths could harm exploration and generalization, a risk not evaluated in the experiments.
- **Narrow Experimental Scope and Scale**
  - Evidence: Section 4.1 evaluates only on AIME24 using a single model (Qwen3-8B). Why it matters: Limits generalizability to other reasoning domains, model architectures, or scales, reducing the breadth of the claimed impact.
  - Evidence: Section 4.1 tests only one staleness level ($S=11$) and does not explore higher staleness regimes where policy drift is more severe. Why it matters: The method's primary motivation is handling staleness; testing a single point leaves its robustness under extreme off-policy conditions unverified.
  - Evidence: Section 4.1 uses only 3 seeds with 12 initial steps, extending just one seed to 60 steps for final evaluation. Why it matters: Small sample size and short training horizons reduce statistical power and make it difficult to assess long-term stability or variance across runs.4) Suggestions for Improvement
- **Strengthen Theoretical Grounding for the Minimum Aggregation**
  - Provide a derivation or citation showing why $\min_{k<t} \rho_k$ bounds gradient variance or bias more effectively than the cumulative product or mean, particularly under policy drift.
  - Analyze the interaction between the prefix minimum operator and the clipping mechanism in Equation 5, demonstrating that the modified ratio preserves trust-region constraints or bounded gradient norms.
  - Include an ablation comparing the minimum operator against alternatives (e.g., geometric mean, exponential moving average, or product ratio) to empirically justify its superiority in stabilizing or improving updates.
- **Clarify Prefix Classification and Dampening Metrics**
  - Explicitly define the threshold or rule used to classify prefixes as "good" or "bad" in Table 2 (e.g., based on trajectory reward, verifier correctness, or a fixed log-ratio cutoff).
  - Specify the exact importance ratio threshold used to count a token as "dampened" in Section 4.3, and report sensitivity of the selectivity ratio to this threshold choice.
  - Add an analysis or ablation examining whether the method inadvertently suppresses correct but low-probability early tokens, and propose a mitigation (e.g., reward-aware prefix weighting) if such cases are observed.
- **Expand Experimental Evaluation and Statistical Robustness**
  - Evaluate the method on additional mathematical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH, or LiveCodeBench) and at least one larger model size to verify cross-domain and cross-scale generalizability.
  - Test across a range of staleness levels (e.g., $S=5, 15, 20$) to demonstrate robustness under increasing policy drift and identify potential performance cliffs or saturation points.
  - Increase the number of random seeds (e.g., to 5 or more) and extend all runs to the full 60-step horizon to provide statistically significant results and better characterize training variance and long-term stability.5) Score

- Novelty (10): 7 — The prefix-aware ratio formulation (Equation 4) adapts existing off-policy intuition to GRPO, providing a clear mechanism for selective gradient dampening, though it builds directly on MinPRO (Section 2).
- Technical Quality (10): 5 — The implementation is straightforward and computationally efficient (Section 3.4), yet the lack of theoretical justification for the minimum operator (Equation 3) and ambiguous selectivity metrics (Table 2) reduce technical rigor.
- Clarity (10): 7 — The manuscript clearly articulates the motivation, method, and results with well-structured figures and tables (Figure 1, Table 1), though a formula inconsistency in the conclusion (Section 5) and undefined thresholds slightly hinder precision.
- Confidence (5): 4 — High confidence based on clear methodological description and reproducible experimental setup (Section 4.1), though limited scale and missing ablation details prevent full verification of claims.
