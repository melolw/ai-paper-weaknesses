1) Summary
The paper addresses the issue of false memory storage in agent memory systems, specifically identifying "assistant-originated self-verification" as a root cause where assistant-generated hallucinations validate themselves against the full dialogue history. The authors propose Speaker-Attested Grounding (SAG), a minimal intervention that restricts the evidence corpus used for memory filtering to user turns only, while maintaining full-dialogue context for extraction. Evaluated on the HaluMem-Medium benchmark (5-user subset), SAG improves False Memory Resistance (FMR) by +11.94 percentage points (relaxed) or +15.66pp (strict) compared to a dialogue-wide baseline, at the cost of a recall decrease of −7.20pp to −9.33pp. Ablation studies attribute approximately 47.5% of the improvement to the speaker restriction mechanism and 52.5% to reduced evidence length.2) Strengths
*   **Clear Identification of a Specific Failure Mode**
    *   The paper effectively isolates "assistant-originated self-verification" as a distinct problem, explaining how standard pipelines fail when assistant statements serve as their own evidence (Section 1, Section 3.1).
    *   This conceptual clarity is supported by the per-source analysis in Figure 2, which shows that 100% of the FMR gains come from assistant-only interference, validating the targeted nature of the solution.
    *   The problem formulation in Section 3.1 clearly distinguishes between the extraction stage (benefiting from full context) and the filtering stage (requiring user attestation), providing a strong theoretical basis for the method.
*   **Rigorous Ablation and Baseline Design**
    *   The authors proactively address the confounding variable of evidence length by introducing a "Token-Matched" baseline (Section 4.4, Table 2).
    *   This ablation study quantitatively isolates the contribution of the speaker restriction (47.5% of gains) from the effect of shorter context windows (52.5%), demonstrating technical thoroughness.
    *   The comparison against the "Extract-Only" baseline in Table 1 highlights the minimal utility of dialogue-wide filtering, reinforcing the necessity of the proposed intervention.
*   **Minimalist and Practical Intervention**
    *   SAG is presented as a single-variable change (Equation 1), making it highly implementable in existing memory pipelines without requiring complex architectural changes or retraining.
    *   The proposal of two variants, "strict" and "relaxed" (Section 3.3), offers practitioners a configurable trade-off between precision and recall, increasing the method's applicability to different use cases.
    *   The pipeline diagram in Figure 1 clearly visualizes the separation between Stage 1 (Extraction) and Stage 2 (Filtering), enhancing the clarity of the implementation details.
*   **Detailed Error Analysis**
    *   The error analysis in Section 4.5 provides valuable insights into the limitations of the method, specifically identifying "semantic overlap" as the primary cause of remaining false positives.
    *   The distinction between false positives (semantic overlap) and false negatives (extraction inaccuracies vs. genuine recall loss) offers a nuanced understanding of where the method succeeds and fails.
    *   This analysis suggests concrete directions for future work, such as combining SAG with semantic deduplication, demonstrating a constructive approach to the method's limitations.3) Weaknesses
*   **Limited Dataset Scale and Generalizability**
    *   The evaluation is conducted on a "5-user subset comprising 343 dialogue sessions" (Section 4.1), which is a very small sample size for drawing broad conclusions about agent memory systems.
    *   The results may not generalize to larger, more diverse datasets with varying dialogue lengths, topics, and user behaviors, limiting the robustness of the claims.
    *   The lack of evaluation on larger benchmarks (e.g., the full HaluMem dataset or other memory benchmarks) makes it difficult to assess the scalability of the method.
*   **Significant Recall Trade-off**
    *   The method incurs a notable drop in recall (−7.20pp for relaxed, −9.33pp for strict) as shown in Table 1, which could be detrimental in applications where capturing all user information is critical.
    *   The paper does not sufficiently analyze the *nature* of the lost memories; it is unclear if the dropped memories are trivial or essential for personalization.
    *   The F1 score drops significantly (from 66.63 to 60.66 for relaxed), suggesting that the gain in FMR may not always outweigh the loss in recall depending on the application's priorities.
*   **Fundamental Limitation Regarding Semantic Overlap**
    *   The error analysis reveals that 100% of remaining false positives involve "semantic overlap," where the user discusses the same topic as the interference memory (Section 4.5).
    *   This indicates a fundamental limitation of the speaker-restriction approach: it cannot distinguish between correct and incorrect details if the user has engaged with the topic, creating an irreducible error floor.
    *   The paper acknowledges this but does not propose a solution within the scope of the work, leaving a significant gap in handling nuanced hallucinations.
*   **Weak Baseline Comparison**
    *   The "Dialogue-Wide" baseline shows minimal improvement over "Extract-Only" (FMR: 58.76% vs 56.90%), suggesting that the baseline itself is weak or poorly optimized (Table 1).
    *   The paper does not compare SAG against other advanced memory filtering or grounding methods mentioned in the Related Work, such as A-MemGuard (Wei et al., 2025) or consensus-based validation.
    *   Without comparison to state-of-the-art filtering techniques, it is difficult to determine if SAG is superior to existing solutions or simply better than a naive baseline.
*   **Critical Numerical Inconsistency in FMR Reporting**
    *   There is a substantial mathematical contradiction between the overall FMR values in Table 1 and the subgroup breakdowns in Figure 2 and Section 4.3.
    *   Table 1 reports the overall FMR for Dialogue-Wide as 58.76%, while Section 4.3 states that the same method achieves 86.65% on assistant-only interference (70.9% of cases) and 80.45% on user-repeated interference (27.8% of cases).
    *   A weighted average of these subgroup scores yields approximately 83.8%, which is irreconcilable with the reported 58.76% overall score, raising serious concerns about data aggregation or metric calculation.4) Suggestions for Improvement
*   **Expand Evaluation Scale**
    *   Evaluate SAG on the full HaluMem dataset or other large-scale memory benchmarks to demonstrate robustness across diverse users and dialogue types.
    *   Include statistics on dialogue length and complexity to show how SAG performs under varying conditions.
    *   Consider cross-validation or user-level splits to ensure the results are not biased by specific user behaviors in the 5-user subset.
*   **Analyze Recall Loss in Detail**
    *   Provide a qualitative analysis of the memories lost due to the recall drop, categorizing them by importance or type (e.g., preferences vs. facts).
    *   Discuss the downstream impact of missing these memories on agent performance or user satisfaction.
    *   Explore if a hybrid approach (e.g., using SAG for high-risk memories and dialogue-wide for others) could mitigate the recall loss while maintaining FMR gains.
*   **Address Semantic Overlap**
    *   Propose a secondary filtering step or a semantic similarity threshold to handle cases where the user discusses the topic but the details are incorrect.
    *   Investigate if combining SAG with fact-checking or consistency checks could reduce the error floor identified in Section 4.5.
    *   Discuss potential prompt engineering strategies to help the LLM verifier distinguish between topic discussion and factual attestation.
*   **Strengthen Baseline Comparisons**
    *   Compare SAG against more advanced baselines, such as A-MemGuard or other grounding-based methods, to establish its relative performance.
    *   Optimize the "Dialogue-Wide" baseline (e.g., by tuning prompts or using stronger verifiers) to ensure a fair comparison.
    *   Include a discussion on how SAG complements or differs from existing security-focused memory frameworks.
*   **Reconcile FMR Calculations and Reporting**
    *   Verify the underlying data aggregation and explicitly reconcile the overall FMR scores in Table 1 with the per-source breakdowns in Figure 2 and Section 4.3.
    *   Clarify whether different subsets of the dataset were used for the overall evaluation versus the per-source analysis, or correct the calculation error if a single dataset was used.
    *   Ensure all reported metrics are mathematically consistent across tables, figures, and text to restore confidence in the empirical claims.5) Score
- Overall (10): 5 — The paper presents a clear intervention for a specific failure mode, but the critical numerical inconsistency between Table 1 and Figure 2/Section 4.3 significantly undermines the empirical claims.
- Novelty (10): 6 — The concept of restricting evidence to user turns is intuitive and effectively formalized, but represents an incremental refinement within established gated-memory paradigms rather than a fundamental breakthrough.
- Technical Quality (10): 4 — While the ablation design is rigorous, the irreconcilable FMR metric discrepancy across Table 1 and Section 4.3 raises serious concerns about data aggregation and experimental validity.
- Clarity (10): 8 — The paper is well-structured and clearly written, though conflicting values between Figure 1, Table 1, and Figure 2 create confusion regarding baseline performance.
- Confidence (5): 4 — High confidence in the methodological clarity, but lower confidence in the reported results due to the unresolved numerical contradictions.
