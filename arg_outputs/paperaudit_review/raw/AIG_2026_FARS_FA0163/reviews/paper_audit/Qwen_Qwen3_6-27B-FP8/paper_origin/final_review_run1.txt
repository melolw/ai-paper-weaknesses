1) Summary
The paper addresses the inefficiency of redundant execution feedback in test-time code scaling, where multiple candidates often fail for similar reasons. The authors propose Execution-Signature Recycling (ESR), a training-free method that clusters candidates by their execution signatures (sets of failing tests and error types) and conditions a second generation round on a deduplicated failure bank. Evaluated on HumanEval+ using Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct under a fixed 16-generation budget, ESR is compared against Best-of-16 and per-sample Self-Debug baselines. While ESR achieves the highest mean Pass@1 (87.60%), the improvement over Self-Debug (86.99%) is not statistically significant, with a 95% confidence interval of [−0.81, 2.24] that includes zero. The authors transparently report this as a negative result, concluding that cross-sample feedback aggregation offers no reliable advantage over simpler per-sample debugging for strong code models on this benchmark.2) Strengths
- **Transparent and Rigorous Reporting of a Negative Result**
  - The Abstract and Section 3.2 explicitly state the non-significant finding and provide the exact 95% confidence interval [−0.81, 2.24], which matters because it prevents publication bias and guides the community away from unnecessary methodological complexity.
  - Pre-registered decision rules are declared in Section 1 and applied in Section 3.2, which matters for experimental rigor by reducing the risk of post-hoc hypothesis testing and p-hacking.
  - The discussion in Section 3.3 honestly analyzes potential causes for the null result (strong base model, high all-pass rate, sufficient per-sample feedback), which matters for impact by providing actionable insights for future test-time scaling research.
- **Precise and Reproducible Methodological Specification**
  - The execution signature definition in Equation (1) and the two-round procedure in Section 2.3 are explicitly parameterized (e.g., $N=16$, $M=3$, $K=2$, 900-token budget), which matters for technical soundness by enabling direct replication and fair comparison.
  - Figure 1 provides a clear visual pipeline of the exploration, aggregation, and exploitation phases, which matters for clarity by helping readers quickly grasp the data flow and feedback conditioning mechanism.
  - The candidate selection criterion (maximizing base-test pass rate, ties broken by fewer failing tests) is consistently stated in Section 2.3 and Section 3.1, which matters for experimental rigor by ensuring uniform evaluation across all methods.
- **Comprehensive Statistical and Diagnostic Analysis**
  - The use of paired bootstrap confidence intervals over 492 task-seed pairs (Section 3.1) provides a robust statistical assessment, which matters for technical soundness by moving beyond simple mean comparisons to account for per-task variance.
  - Table 2 reveals that ~68% of tasks yield empty failure banks, which matters for impact by directly contextualizing the limited applicability of the proposed mechanism and explaining the null result.
  - Variance analysis in Section 3.3 highlights ESR’s higher inter-seed standard deviation (1.27 vs. 0.70 for Self-Debug), which matters for experimental rigor by demonstrating thorough evaluation of method stability and reliability.3) Weaknesses
- **Limited Generalizability Due to Narrow Experimental Scope**
  - Evaluation is restricted to a single model (Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct) and a single benchmark (HumanEval+), as noted in Section 3.1, which matters for impact because conclusions about ESR’s effectiveness cannot be extended to different model scales, architectures, or more complex code synthesis tasks.
  - The fixed budget of 16 generations (Section 2.3) is relatively small, which matters for technical soundness because test-time scaling methods often exhibit different behaviors under larger budgets (e.g., 32 or 64), leaving a critical scaling regime unexplored.
  - No ablation studies are provided to isolate the impact of key hyperparameters (e.g., $M=3$, $K=2$, token budget), which matters for experimental rigor because it remains unclear how sensitive ESR is to these design choices or whether they are optimally tuned.
- **Suboptimal Failure Bank Construction and Clustering Strategy**
  - Exact signature equality clustering (Section 2.3) is overly rigid, which matters for technical soundness because candidates failing on the same test with semantically similar but differently formatted error messages would be split into separate clusters, fragmenting useful feedback.
  - The failure bank truncates to the top-3 clusters and 2 examples each (Section 2.3), which matters for impact because it may discard valuable minority failure modes that are critical for resolving edge cases or rare bugs.
  - No direct evidence is provided regarding how the 900-token budget is enforced or whether prompt formatting affects model comprehension of the failure bank, which matters for reproducibility and clarity.
- **Insufficient Empirical Analysis of the Negative Result’s Underlying Causes**
  - While Section 3.3 speculates on reasons for the null result, it lacks empirical validation (e.g., correlation analysis between failure bank size and performance gain), which matters for technical soundness because hypotheses remain unverified by data.
  - The high variance of ESR (std=1.27 in Table 1) is noted but not deeply investigated, which matters for experimental rigor because it is unclear whether this stems from prompt sensitivity, clustering instability, or task-specific characteristics.
  - The comparison baseline (Self-Debug) uses per-sample feedback for up to 3 failing tests (Section 3.1), while ESR uses up to 2 examples per cluster, which matters for experimental rigor because this asymmetry in feedback granularity is not addressed and may confound the comparison.
- **Statistical Reporting and Numerical Inconsistencies**
  - The reported mean differences do not align with the centers of the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (Section 3.2, Table 1); for ESR vs. Self-Debug, the mean difference is +0.61 but the CI [−0.81, 2.24] centers at 0.715, which matters for technical soundness as it suggests a calculation mismatch or rounding artifact that undermines statistical precision.
  - In-text citations reference "Wang et al., 2026" (Sections 2.4 and 4), while the reference list correctly lists the publication year as 2025, which matters for clarity and proofreading rigor.
  - Per-seed performance differences in Section 3.3 (+2.44pp, −0.61pp) slightly mismatch the exact arithmetic from Table 1 values (+2.43, −0.60), which matters for reproducibility and attention to detail.4) Suggestions for Improvement
- **Expand Experimental Scope and Conduct Ablation Studies**
  - Evaluate ESR on additional benchmarks (e.g., MBPP, LiveCodeBench) and model sizes (e.g., 1.5B, 14B) to assess generalizability beyond the current setup in Section 3.1.
  - Test the method under larger generation budgets (e.g., 32, 64) to determine if cross-sample aggregation yields benefits when more candidates are available for clustering, addressing the fixed budget limitation in Section 2.3.
  - Perform hyperparameter ablations on $M$, $K$, and the token budget to identify optimal configurations and demonstrate robustness, directly addressing the missing ablation studies.
- **Refine Clustering and Failure Bank Construction**
  - Replace exact signature matching with semantic or structural similarity metrics (e.g., error message embedding similarity or AST-based traceback comparison) to group related failures more effectively, mitigating the rigidity noted in Section 2.3.
  - Investigate adaptive bank sizing or priority-based selection instead of fixed top-3 clustering, ensuring critical but less frequent failure modes are retained, addressing the truncation limitation in Section 2.3.
  - Provide detailed prompt templates and token allocation strategies for the failure bank to ensure reproducibility and clarify how the 900-token limit is managed, filling the missing implementation details.
- **Deepen Empirical Analysis of the Null Result and Variance**
  - Conduct case studies or error analysis on tasks where ESR outperforms or underperforms Self-Debug to identify specific failure patterns or task characteristics that drive performance differences, validating the hypotheses in Section 3.3.
  - Analyze the relationship between failure bank utilization (e.g., number of non-empty banks, cluster sizes from Table 2) and Pass@1 gains to quantify when cross-sample feedback is actually beneficial, providing empirical grounding for the discussion.
  - Align feedback granularity between baselines and ESR (e.g., standardize the number of failing test examples provided) to ensure a fair comparison and isolate the impact of cross-sample aggregation, addressing the asymmetry noted in Section 3.1.
- **Verify Statistical Calculations and Proofread References**
  - Recalculate the paired bootstrap confidence intervals to ensure their centers align with the reported mean differences in Table 1 and Section 3.2, or explicitly clarify the statistical method causing the offset.
  - Correct the citation year for Wang et al. in Sections 2.4 and 4 to match the reference list (2025), ensuring bibliographic consistency.
  - Reconcile the per-seed difference calculations in Section 3.3 with the exact values presented in Table 1 to eliminate minor rounding discrepancies and improve numerical precision.5) Score
- Overall (10): 4 — The paper presents a transparent negative result with rigorous statistical reporting (Section 3.2, Table 1), but the narrow experimental scope, rigid clustering, and newly identified numerical inconsistencies limit its broader impact and trustworthiness.
- Novelty (10): 6 — The idea of deduplicating execution feedback via signature clustering (Section 2.2, Equation 1) is conceptually clear, though it builds directly on existing self-debugging and experience recycling paradigms.
- Technical Quality (10): 5 — The methodology is well-specified and statistically sound in design (Section 3.1, Table 2), but the lack of ablations, hyperparameter sensitivity analysis, and the mismatch between mean differences and CI centers (Section 3.2) reduce technical depth and precision.
- Clarity (10): 7 — The manuscript is well-structured, with clear definitions, a helpful pipeline figure (Figure 1), and transparent reporting of results, though minor citation and rounding inconsistencies (Sections 2.4, 3.3, References) detract from polish.
- Confidence (5): 4 — High confidence due to clear methodology, explicit statistical tests, and reproducible setup, though limited by the single-model/single-benchmark evaluation and minor numerical discrepancies.
