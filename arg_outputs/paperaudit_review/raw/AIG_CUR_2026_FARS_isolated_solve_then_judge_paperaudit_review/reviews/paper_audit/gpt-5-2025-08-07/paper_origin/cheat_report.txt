Academic integrity and internal consistency risk report

Major issues (with direct anchors to the manuscript)

1) Ambiguity/inconsistency about which attack variant underlies the “main results” in Table 1 and the Abstract
- Evidence:
  - Section 4.1 (Experimental Setup, Attack): “We evaluate three prompt injection variants...”
  - Section 4.2: “Table 1 presents the main experimental results comparing all three conditions.” Table 1 lists ASR_cond for A=91.33%, B=29.33%, C=33.37%.
  - Table 3/Figure 2 report per-variant ASR_cond for A: Primary=91.3, Authority=88.6, Verdict=74.7; for B: Primary=29.0, Authority=63.6, Verdict=39.0.
- Problem:
  - If Table 1 were aggregating across the three variants introduced in Section 4.1, the baseline ASR_cond could not exceed the highest single-variant baseline (91.3% for Primary). Yet Table 1 reports A ASR_cond=91.33%, which is slightly higher than the Primary variant alone and far above the simple average across variants (mean of 91.3, 88.6, 74.7 ≈ 84.9%). This implies Table 1 is not an average across all variants despite the text implying comprehensive evaluation, and likely reflects (primarily) the Primary variant.
  - The Abstract cites “reduces conditional attack success rate from 91.3% to 29.3%,” which numerically matches Table 1 (91.33→29.33) and closely tracks Table 3’s Primary numbers (91.3→29.0), but the manuscript never states explicitly that the headline numbers correspond to the Primary variant only.
- Why it matters:
  - The headline 62pp reduction is variant-dependent; presenting it as the “main” or overall result without clarifying the variant risks overstating effectiveness relative to the average across attacks.

2) Sample size/denominator inconsistencies that affect interpretability and reproducibility
- Evidence:
  - Dataset size: N=1,247 (Section 4.1).
  - Section 4.5: “In Condition C, 1.88% (23/1,224) of Pass 1 outputs show corruption.”
  - Section 4.6: “Analysis of 238 attack successes against Condition B...”
  - Table 1 (Condition B): CleanAcc=67.52%, ASR_cond=29.33%.
- Problems:
  - The denominator in Section 4.5 is 1,224, not the stated N=1,247. No explanation is provided for the missing 23 cases or the choice of denominator (“23/1,224”).
  - The count of “238 attack successes” for Condition B is inconsistent with Table 1’s rates if applied to N=1,247. Using Table 1, the expected number of ASR_cond successes would be approximately 0.6752 × 1,247 × 0.2933 ≈ 247 (not 238). No filtering or alternative denominator is specified to reconcile this 9-case discrepancy.
- Why it matters:
  - Unexplained denominator changes create uncertainty about which subset each analysis uses, undermining the claims about effect sizes and failure modes.

3) Unclear attack indexing (which response is attacked) conflicts with multiple places that appear to fix the attack to Response 2
- Evidence:
  - Section 3.1 gives an example “R2 ← R2 ⊕ A” and Section 3.3 Eq. (4) explicitly evaluates “R2 ⊕ A” in Pass 2.
  - Figure 1 (Section 3.4) depicts injection on Response 2 across Conditions A–C.
  - Section 4.1 says attacks target “the human-dispreferred response,” which could be either R1 or R2 depending on the instance.
  - Section 4.6: “98.7% of failures occurred when the attacked response was in position 2 (idx=1).”
- Problems:
  - The paper repeatedly formalizes and visualizes the attack as applied to R2, while textually claiming the target is the “human-dispreferred response” (not necessarily R2). It is not stated whether the injected instruction’s “Response X” dynamically matches the actual dispreferred candidate’s index on each example.
- Why it matters:
  - If the injection is effectively always on R2, the observed “98.7% when attacked response was in position 2” may simply reflect design rather than a learned position bias. This materially affects interpretation of the bias analysis and the generality of the defense.

4) Incomplete metric definitions and comparability across conditions
- Evidence:
  - Metrics (Section 4.1): “ASR—overall attack success rate; ASR_cond—attack success rate conditioned on the model originally selecting the correct (non-attacked) response.”
  - Tables 1–3 report ASR and ASR_cond but do not specify whether the ASR_cond denominator is harmonized across conditions (e.g., using a common set) or computed per-condition on potentially different subsets.
- Problems:
  - Without a clear statement on whether ASR_cond denominators are the same across A, B, C (or per-variant), cross-condition differences (e.g., the claimed +4pp benefit of isolation vs. prompt engineering, Section 4.2) could be confounded by denominator shifts.
  - Per-category breakdown in Table 2 lists only ASR_cond, without the per-category denominators used for conditioning, further obscuring comparability.
- Why it matters:
  - The central claim that isolation provides an additional 4pp benefit beyond prompt engineering relies on fair cross-condition comparability. The manuscript does not provide the necessary denominator details to verify this.

5) Missing methodological details that are necessary to validate the claimed mechanism (“information isolation”) and to reproduce results
- Evidence:
  - Section 3.4 mentions Pass 2 uses “data delimiters and explicit warnings about potential injection content,” but the exact prompts for Pass 1 and Pass 2 are not provided anywhere in the manuscript. No direct evidence found in the manuscript.
  - Section 4.5 defines “corruption” via “verdict markers or injection-related content,” but the detection heuristic/regex/criteria are not specified. No direct evidence found in the manuscript.
- Why it matters:
  - The claim that information isolation (B vs C) adds ~4pp robustness “beyond prompt engineering” cannot be independently assessed without exact prompts and corruption-detection definitions. These omissions materially affect reproducibility and the strength of causal attribution.

6) Causal interpretation not directly supported by quantitative evidence
- Evidence:
  - Section 4.5 concludes: “since most attacks succeed via Pass 2 manipulation rather than Pass 1 corruption, the primary defense mechanism is the uncontaminated reference anchor...”
- Problem:
  - No quantitative breakdown is provided attributing a majority of failures to Pass 2 vs Pass 1 pathways beyond the small Pass 1 corruption rate and two conditional rates (73.9% vs 47.8%). The direct evidence isolating Pass 2 as the primary cause of attack success is not shown. No direct evidence found in the manuscript.
- Why it matters:
  - The mechanistic claim is central to the paper’s thesis; without explicit evidence, the conclusion remains unsubstantiated.

Minor/rounding notes (do not materially affect conclusions)
- Section 4.2 cites “74.8pp reduction” for Hallucination; Table 2 shows 95.9→21.2 (−74.7pp). Likely rounding.

Actionable clarifications requested
- Explicitly state which attack variant(s) Table 1 and the Abstract statistics refer to, and if they are averaged, provide the aggregation method and per-variant weights.
- Reconcile denominators across sections: explain why Section 4.5 uses 1,224 instead of N=1,247 and how the 238 successes in Section 4.6 relate to the rates in Table 1.
- Specify whether the injection index (“Response X”) dynamically matches the human-dispreferred response per example; if not, report the distribution of attacked indices and re-interpret the position-bias finding accordingly.
- Define ASR and ASR_cond precisely, including denominators and whether they are harmonized across conditions and categories. Provide the per-category denominators used for Table 2.
- Provide the exact prompts for Pass 1 and Pass 2 for Conditions A, B, and C, and the heuristic used to label “Pass 1 corruption.”
- If claiming Pass 2 is the dominant failure pathway, include quantitative evidence directly supporting that attribution.

Conclusion
Based on the manuscript, there are clear internal inconsistencies and missing methodological details that materially affect the interpretation and credibility of the results: (1) mismatch/ambiguity about which attack variant underlies the headline “main results,” (2) unexplained denominator and sample-size inconsistencies, and (3) unclear/possibly fixed attack indexing that conflicts with the stated procedure. These issues should be resolved to ensure the findings are valid, comparable, and reproducible.
