# Expected Review Outcome:

## Paper Summary
The paper investigates how to allocate a fixed budget of world-model queries when verifying irreversible actions (checkout) in a web agent setting. It argues that sequential verify-and-retry can degrade task success as budget increases due to trajectory drift: when a checkout is blocked, the agent re-browses and often picks a worse product. The proposed remedy is chunked budget allocation, using fewer verification cycles with more parallel world-model rollouts per cycle (extreme case M=1). On WebShop with budget B=10, chunking with no aggregation (M=1, K=10, use first rollout) reports 21.50% success, compared to 0.86% for sequential. The paper also observes that consensus aggregation over parallel rollouts brings little benefit because the world model is poorly calibrated, with most cycles predicting failure.

## Summary Of Strengths
- Problem framing: The paper isolates a concrete and practically relevant failure mode in web-agent verification, namely trajectory drift induced by repeated re-browsing after blocked checkouts. The narrative about “structure of allocation” vs. “aggregation sophistication” is crisp and actionable.
- Clear experimental knob: Fixing total budget B=10 and varying cycle count M vs. per-cycle rollouts K offers a focused comparison (Sections 3.3 and 4.3).
- Strong headline contrast: Table 1 shows a very large gap between sequential (A) and chunked (C), and Figure 2 visualizes the monotonic degradation as M increases, making the core claim easy to interpret.
- Mechanism-oriented analysis: Table 2 provides a failure taxonomy, attributing 60.5% of wins of chunked over sequential to trajectory drift. This is a useful qualitative anchor for practitioners designing agent loops.
- Negative result on aggregation: The study that consensus adds little when the world model is severely miscalibrated is worth documenting. Section 4.6 and Eq. (1) make the evaluation logic explicit. This discourages over-investing in sophisticated voting when the calibration regime is hostile.
- Figure-level strength: Figure 1 concisely contrasts the control flow for sequential vs. chunked verification, emphasizing where trajectory drift can be introduced or curtailed. It adds clarity to the pipeline difference beyond text.

## Summary Of Weaknesses
1) Scope limited to a single domain and model pairing
- All claims are demonstrated only on WebShop with Gemini-2.5-Flash as the actor and a fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B as the world model (Section 4.1). While WebShop is standard, the generalization of the claimed “structure dominates aggregation” result to other irreversible-action domains (email/robotics/other web benchmarks) is untested. This weakens the strength of the conclusions.

2) Absolute performance is modest and stability unclear
- The best reported success is 21.50% (Table 1, Condition C). Although the relative gain is large, this level limits practical impact. The paper does not analyze variance across categories (price constraints, attribute counts) or per-intent difficulty, so it is unclear whether gains are concentrated in specific subsets.

3) Decision rules differ across conditions and may confound comparisons
- Condition A uses a single rollout with a binary R′ check; Condition B uses consensus with τ=0.1; Condition C uses the first rollout without aggregation while still spawning K=10 rollouts (Section 3.3). This mixes changes in cycle count, rollout multiplicity, and decision threshold. A clean comparison would hold the acceptance rule fixed while sweeping only M and K. As written, it is hard to attribute gains purely to “fewer cycles” rather than to differences in gating criteria and rollout generation.

4) Fairness and mechanics of “budget exhaustion” need tighter specification
- The framework states that after M cycles, subsequent checkout attempts auto-pass (Section 3.2). For M=1, this still permits one re-browse before an automatic checkout, which partially reintroduces drift. The paper asserts that chunking “eliminates” sequential cycles but does not quantify how often auto-pass occurs after a failed M=1 check or how that affects outcomes. A per-episode timeline analysis would help.

5) Calibration analysis incomplete and somewhat inconsistent
- Section 4.6 reports that in Condition B, 98.4% of cycles have hat p=0 with an actual success rate of 27.5% when hat p=0. This is a striking miscalibration. But the paper does not provide calibration curves, reliability diagrams, or an evaluation of simple fixes (e.g., temperature scaling, Platt scaling). Given that aggregation’s failure is central, it is important to test whether minimal calibration improves B. Also, the basis for 84/306 “successes” when hat p=0 needs clarification: success at episode level or action-level verifications per cycle? If most cycles output 0 yet episodes succeed, that suggests the downstream auto-pass or later dynamics dominate, which complicates the causal story.

6) Missing ablations that would strengthen causal claims
- Threshold sensitivity: Only τ=0.1 is used in Condition B’s main result. Figure 2 mentions τ=0.5 in a different setup, but there is no systematic sweep of τ across M,K to demonstrate robustness. Since consensus and τ interact, the conclusions about aggregation could change under different τ.
- Actor temperature and policy stability: The agent acts at temperature 1.0 (Section 4.1). If re-browsing drift is policy-stochasticity driven, reducing actor temperature may reduce drift and narrow A vs. C. An ablation is warranted.
- Alternative acceptance rules that keep M constant (e.g., M=1 with single rollout vs. K>1 but accept-any, majority, unanimity) would help isolate the effect of parallelism vs. cycle reduction.

7) Baselines and prior art engagement are thin for verification design
- The paper frames verification strictly as “querying a learned world model” and deciding pass/block, but it does not compare against or discuss recent verification frameworks that target reliability through model structure or predicate/event verification rather than compute allocation. This weakens positioning and makes it hard to judge whether chunking is complementary or a substitute.

8) Reproducibility and openness concerns
- The acting agent is a proprietary model. The world model is a fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B, but hyperparameters, data splits for fine-tuning, and prompts are not detailed. Section 4.1 mentions CI computation but does not provide the exact bootstrap procedure (paired vs. unpaired, episode alignment). Without prompts and seeds for the actor/world model, reproducing the key numbers (e.g., Table 1) will be difficult.

9) Statistical reporting gaps
- Table 1 provides CIs on success rates, and the text reports a paired bootstrap CI for C−A, but there is no disclosure of whether pairing used per-task identities across seeds or whether episodes are aligned across conditions. Given 600 episodes/condition, the dependency structure matters. Also, the “non-monotonic” claim in Section 4.3 (M=2 worse than M=5) is not accompanied by significance tests.

10) Claims occasionally overreach evidence
- The Abstract and Conclusion suggest “spending all budget in one parallel chunk consistently outperforms finer-grained allocations,” yet Figure 2 is presented for one B with one set of actor/WMs and limited τ/K regimes. “Consistently” is stronger than the data supports. Similarly, “consensus provides no benefit” depends on a single poorly calibrated world model; this should be qualified.

Figure- and Table-specific critiques
- Figure 1: The diagrams are clear and useful. However, the right panel suggests “Reduced distribution shift” when chunking, but the mechanics still permit at least one re-browse for M=1 after a block. The figure could overstate elimination of drift rather than reduction of opportunities for drift.
- Figure 2: The bar chart shows a big spread from M=1 to M=10, aligning with the main thesis. Yet the error bars overlap for M=2 vs. M=5, so the text’s discussion of “non-monotonic” behavior should avoid implying significance without tests.
- Table 1: The largest number is the no-aggregation chunked variant (C) outperforming consensus (B) by 6.67 pp. This is counterintuitive and central, but the table does not indicate whether C−B is significant. Given the paper’s emphasis on aggregation failing due to calibration, a significance line for C−B would strengthen the argument.
- Table 2: The failure taxonomy is helpful, but the coding criteria for “trajectory drift” vs. “wrong product checkout” are not operationalized. Are these mutually exclusive? Provide labeling guidelines and inter-annotator checks.
- Table 3: The temperature-0 result is insightful; with identical rollouts, any advantage from “diverse sampling” disappears, yet chunking still helps, which supports the structural thesis. However, these results are for M=2, K=5, τ=0.5 only. Including M=1, K=10 at temp=0 would tighten the story.

## Comments Suggestions And Typos
Actionable suggestions
- Provide a controlled comparison that holds the acceptance rule fixed: e.g., set a single acceptance criterion across A, B, C (same τ and same binary mapping of R′) while only changing M and K. This would directly test the “cycle reduction” hypothesis.
- Calibrate the world model and revisit consensus: Try simple calibration (temperature scaling, Platt/Isotonic) on held-out WebShop-like trajectories, then re-run B to test whether aggregation becomes helpful once probabilities are meaningful. Add a reliability diagram and report ECE/Brier before and after calibration.
- Make auto-pass effects explicit: For M=1, report the fraction of episodes where the first cycle blocks and the episode later succeeds due to auto-pass on a subsequent checkout. Provide timelines showing whether chunking truly prevents or merely reduces drift.
- Add sensitivity analyses: Sweep τ across a grid for multiple M,K; vary actor temperature; include an oracle stop rule (limit the number of re-browse cycles for A) to decouple drift from miscalibration.
- Strengthen failure taxonomy: Define and illustrate “trajectory drift” vs. “wrong product checkout” with concrete criteria and 2–3 annotated examples. Report annotation protocol and agreement.
