# Expected Review Outcome:

## Paper Summary
This paper introduces QRMeM, a “Question then Reflection” memory mechanism to help short‑context LLMs reason over long documents. The approach constructs a dual memory pool: (i) query‑oriented structured memory as a graph with entities and open relations built by LLM prompting and question‑generation, and (ii) a long‑term static memory of original segments to avoid error propagation. It then proposes three navigation strategies for selecting relevant segments: ENTITY TRIAL, GRAPH EXPANSION SEARCH, and the full reflection‑guided QRMeM that iteratively expands via edges deemed relevant given the current failure reason. Experiments on QuALITY (MCQ) and Multi‑Doc QA (HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue, all re‑framed into long documents) report improvements over retrieval baselines and long‑context models, with ablations indicating contributions from graph updates, open‑entity extraction, reflection, and navigation.

## Summary Of Strengths
- Problem framing: Targets the persistent gap between long‑window LLMs and practical long‑context reasoning, and motivates the need for query‑oriented memory rather than static indices.
- Method design: The dual memory pool is sensible, combining structured guidance with raw segments to mitigate information loss. The reflection loop that reuses failure reasons to steer next hops is a reasonable design choice.
- Figures and algorithmic clarity: Figure 2 conveys the two‑stage graph construction and global merging process; Algorithm 1 provides a readable high‑level description of the reflection‑based navigation loop.
- Empirical signal: Table 1 shows the proposed navigation strategies generally outperform standard retrieval and several long‑context baselines on QuALITY and across Multi‑Doc QA sets. The ablation in Table 2 supports the claim that both reflection and query‑oriented graph updates matter, especially for difficult QuALITY examples. Figure 4 shows accuracy improves with more trials, supporting the utility of iterative navigation.
- Analysis of long‑context factors: Section 3.7 and Table 3 plus Figure 5 provide a compact study of position and distance effects among supporting segments, connecting to the “lost in the middle” literature and reinforcing the motivation for re‑organizing context.

## Summary Of Weaknesses
1) Underspecified graph construction and reliability of LLM‑extracted structure (Sections 2.1–2.2; Figure 2). Key steps are left vague:
   - Entity extraction: “OPEN ENTITY refers to the entity recognition method through LLM” (Table 2 caption) but there is no precise prompt, post‑processing, or disambiguation protocol in the main paper. How are coreference and aliasing handled beyond “entity disambiguation” one‑liner? How are errors mitigated when LLM hallucinations create spurious nodes/edges?
   - Relation extraction uses free‑form “descriptive relation representations” generated by an LLM (Page 3, col. 1–2), which risks semantic drift. How are duplicate or contradictory relations merged, and how is noise filtered? The “ROUGE‑L<0.6” threshold for adding questions is not justified, and there is no sensitivity analysis for this hyperparameter.
   - Cost: Query‑oriented graph initialization involves per‑segment entity/edge prompting, plus question generation and later merges. The paper provides no end‑to‑end latency, token, or dollar‑cost breakdown, nor a comparison to strong retrievers with rerankers under similar budgets. Limitation 1 acknowledges cost but does not quantify it.

2) Missing strong baselines and incomplete comparative positioning.
   - The evaluation omits recent memory/RAG systems that embody explicit read‑write or global memory and reflection, which are directly comparable to a dual‑pool design (see “Potentially Missing Related Work”).
   - Retrieval baselines are relatively weak: only BM25 and Contriever, with “top‑3” truncation chosen because “performance does not improve” (Appendix B, Table 4). However, Table 4 shows BM25(Max) and Contriever(max) sometimes improve over top‑3; truncating at 3 seems arbitrary and may understate retrieval‑augmented performance. There is no dense‑retrieval + reranker baseline or graph‑aware retrieval baseline.
   - MemWalker is included but reproduced; details of reimplementation fidelity are not given, and its surprisingly low F1 on Hotpot (Table 1) deserves discussion.

3) Dataset choices and evaluation scope are limited.
   - QuALITY is evaluated on a small subset of 222 examples (Section 3.3) without explaining selection criteria or whether difficulty distribution matches the full benchmark. This raises concerns about variance and cherry‑picking. No statistical significance testing is reported.
   - Multi‑Doc QA setup is adapted from LongBench; Section 3.3 states average length >8k, but the exact protocol for constructing the long document, chunk sizes, and how answer spans are preserved across concatenations is not fully detailed in the main paper. Section 3.7 later runs additional position/distance probes only on Vicuna variants (Table 3, Figure 5), which limits generality.

4) Fairness of comparisons to long‑context models is not deeply analyzed.
   - Long‑context models in Table 1 are used as single‑shot readers without best‑practice prompt engineering or retrieval‑assisted inputs. It is unclear whether gains come primarily from better selection of short contexts versus inherent modeling benefits. A stronger experimental control would supply the same curated segments to long‑context models or run them with best‑effort retrieval/reranking to isolate the contribution of “navigation” rather than just “shorter, cleaner input.”

5) Clarity and internal consistency issues.
   - In Section 3.4, the discussion paragraph repeats “GPT-3.5-turbo” twice in a confusing comparison (“The reason GPT-3.5-turbo outperforms GPT-3.5-turbo might be...”), making the intended comparison unclear.
   - Naming inconsistencies: “QRMEM” vs “QRMeM” vary across sections and tables. Abbreviations “KN” appears in Table 1 for the main method but is defined as “QRMEM” in text; “TE” for ENTITY TRIAL and “GES” for GRAPH EXPANSION SEARCH are only briefly expanded.
   - References include multiple garbled entries and duplicated author names (Pages 9–11), which complicates provenance checks.

6) Reflection signal and similarity computation details are opaque (Section 2.2; Figure 3).
   - The method ranks edges by similarity between [Q; R; Es] and each edge description using Contriever, but there are no details on encoding edges, whether relation texts are concatenated with entity names, how negatives are handled, or what thresholds are used; Algorithm 1’s Step 10 is high‑level only.
   - The filtering step when the window exceeds L (Algorithm 1, Steps 17–19) depends on S but does not specify tie‑breaking, deduplication, or diversity controls, which matter when many segments are semantically similar.

7) Efficiency/compute trade‑offs are not measured.
   - Figure 4 shows that more trials improve QuALITY accuracy, but there are no runtime or token‑count curves. Without budget curves, it is hard to weigh gains against costs relative to, say, rerankers or more capable long‑context models.

8) Limited analysis of failure modes and qualitative evidence.
   - Only one case study (Table 5) is provided. There is no qualitative error taxonomy for when the reflection picks wrong edges or when open‑IE relations drift. No analysis of entity ambiguity cases or multi‑document co‑reference, which are central to the method.

9) Some experimental choices are potentially confounding.
   - Section 3.2 sets fixed segment size 600 across datasets; there is no sensitivity analysis on chunk size, which strongly interacts with entity extraction, graph density, and retrieval.
   - For HotpotQA, the paper notes “fewer inference steps” versus MuSiQue, but provides no explicit step counts or hop distances for sampled questions; it is hard to attribute differences to “complexity” versus construction artifacts.

10) Minor but notable issues with figures and tables:
   - Figure 3 is informative but dense. The mapping from “Reason R” to next‑edge choice is visualized with scores, but the paper never explicitly specifies how those scores are normalized or how ties are handled. Still, Figure 3 does help the reader grasp the intended control flow, which is a strength; the lack of matching quantitative spec is a weakness.
   - Table 1 mixes many families of models and settings; using both accuracy and EM/F1 is appropriate, but there are no confidence intervals, and bold/underline marking of winners is done per “block,” not globally, which is fine but should be clarified in the caption.

Overall, while the idea is interesting and results are promising, the paper falls short in baseline coverage, methodological transparency, and cost/fairness analysis needed for a main‑track acceptance.

## Potentially Missing Related Work
1) Modarressi et al., “MemLLM: Finetuning LLMs to Use An Explicit Read-Write Memory,” 2024 — introduces explicit read–write external memory with interpretable operations; directly relevant to your dual‑memory pool and reflection updates. Discuss in Section 4.1–4.2 as an alternative memory control scheme, and consider as a baseline or at least a positioning point.

2) Wang et al., “MEMORYLLM: Towards Self-Updatable Large Language Models,” 2024 — proposes a latent memory pool updated over time; compare in Related Work (Section 4.1) and discuss differences between latent vs. graph‑structured, query‑oriented memory.

3) Qian et al., “MemoRAG: Moving towards Next-Gen RAG Via Memory-Inspired Knowledge Discovery,” 2024 — a dual‑system RAG with long‑term memory that discovers and consolidates knowledge; should be contrasted in Section 4 and, if feasible, included as a memory‑augmented RAG baseline given the multi‑document QA setting.

4) Wang et al., “R^3Mem: Bridging Memory Retention and Retrieval via Reversible Compression,” 2025 — a reversible compression memory that jointly optimizes retention and retrieval; relevant to your goal of minimizing information loss. Cite in Section 4.1 and position against your static‑segment safeguard.

5) Terranova et al., “Evaluating Long-Term Memory for Long-Context Question Answering,” 2025 — LoCoMo benchmark for assessing memory strategies; add to Section 3.3/4 as a relevant evaluation resource to contextualize your benchmarks and analysis of memory types.

6) Tavakoli et al., “Beyond a Million Tokens: Benchmarking and Enhancing Long-Term Memory in LLMs,” 2025 — BEAM benchmark and a cognitive‑inspired memory framework. Discuss in Section 4 regarding scaling to ultra‑long contexts and as a potential stress‑test for QRMeM.

7) Mehta et al., “MeMo: Memory as a Model,” 2026 — a modular “memory as a model” design; relate in Section 4.1–4.2 as an architectural alternative to your dual‑pool construction with graph guidance.

## Comments Suggestions And Typos
Actionable questions and suggestions:
- Quantify cost. Please provide per‑dataset statistics: number of segments, average nodes/edges per segment, number of generated questions retained (post‑ROUGE filter), total LLM tokens for construction and for navigation per query, and wall‑clock/runtime. A budget vs. accuracy curve would make Figure 4 much more informative.
- Clarify edge similarity scoring. In Algorithm 1 Step 10 and Figure 3, specify exactly how edges are encoded for Contriever, how [Q; R; Es] is formed, whether scores are normalized, and how ties and exploration–exploitation trade‑offs are handled.
- Strengthen baselines. Add at least one strong RAG+reranker pipeline and one recent memory‑augmented baseline (e.g., a read–write external memory system). For retrieval baselines, report results for Max as well as top‑k, or better yet, equalize prompt length budgets across methods.
- Expand evaluation. Justify the 222‑example QuALITY subset and report confidence intervals or significance tests. Consider adding another long‑document benchmark or a controlled synthetic setup to isolate entity ambiguity and multi‑hop depth.
- Analyze noise and failure modes. Provide a qualitative analysis showing typical LLM‑relation errors, disambiguation mistakes, and cases where reflection helps vs. hurts. A small ablation that toggles relation text granularity or filters noisy edges would be valuable.

## Limitations And Societal Impact
The paper briefly lists computational cost and segmentation impacts. It should also discuss risks from LLM‑constructed graphs, including error propagation, hallucinated relations, and potential bias amplification when entity extraction fails on under‑represented names or domains. As the system reorganizes content, provenance and traceability matter; adding citations to original segments in answers and logging traversal paths would improve transparency. Dual‑use risks are limited but misinterpretation in high‑stakes domains (legal/medical) could be harmful; recommend scoping statements and guardrails.
