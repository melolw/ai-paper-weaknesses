## Paper Summary
This paper studies a failure mode of multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) that the authors call “instinctive bias,” where an image that is semantically related to a question but inconsistent with the correct answer induces hallucinated responses. The paper introduces CorrelationQA, a benchmark of 7,308 image–question pairs across 13 categories. Each question comes with one factual image, five spurious images aligned with incorrect alternatives, and typography (OCR-like) variants. The dataset is generated via GPT‑4 for QA pairs and Stable Diffusion or search engine retrieval for images. The paper evaluates nine representative MLLMs and reports consistent accuracy drops when spurious images are present, especially for typography. The work defines accuracy and an accuracy drop (AccDrop) metric, analyzes sensitivity by category, and provides qualitative examples.

## Summary Of Strengths
- Problem framing and resource contribution:
  - Clear articulation of a practically important failure mode in MLLMs where visually correlated but answer-contradictory images sway the model. The dataset targets a gap not covered by simple VQA.
  - CorrelationQA is sizable (Table 1), spans diverse categories, and includes both natural and OCR-like typography stimuli, enabling controlled comparisons of different spurious-image regimes.
- Evaluation scope and analysis:
  - Broad evaluation across nine MLLMs, including both open and closed models, providing a useful cross-model diagnosis.
  - The evaluation disentangles image conditions: factual, spurious, random, and text-only. Figure 5 is particularly informative in showing that accuracy is comparable in text-only and factual-image settings, but degrades under spurious and especially typography conditions.
  - Category-wise analysis reveals meaningful patterns. Tables 2 and 3 show AccDrop by category, suggesting tangible-object categories (animal/color/plant/food) are more susceptible while concept-heavy categories (history/art) are less so.
- Methodological clarity and visual communication:
  - The dataset generation pipeline in Figure 3 is clearly described and helps readers understand the construction and types of images considered.
  - Aggregate results in Figure 4 give a straightforward view of Acc and AccDrop for each model and image type. The consistent green bars (AccDrop) corroborate the core claim across models.
- Qualitative insights:
  - Figure 6 offers clear qualitative cases where spurious images lead multiple MLLMs astray, which supports the quantitative findings.

Specific figure/table engagement:
- Figure 4: The left panel (natural images) shows, for example, GPT‑4V achieving high factual accuracy but a noticeable green AccDrop bar, visually validating the central claim that even strong models are vulnerable under spurious images. The right panel (typography) reveals much larger green bars for many models, highlighting the outsized impact of OCR-like spurious signals.
- Table 4: The realistic vs. synthetic comparison indicates patterns on synthetic images transfer to retrieved real images for LLaVA‑7B and Qwen‑VL, supporting external validity.
- Table 9: The manual authenticity check provides some sanity verification for GPT‑4-generated questions and answers, with most categories above 90% authenticity.

## Summary Of Weaknesses
1) Scope limitation of task design and answer space
- The benchmark restricts answers to short entities and instructs models to answer in ≤5 words (Section 2.3, Page 4; prompt on Page 5). This narrows the phenomenon to a specific QA style, which weakens claims about “instinctive bias” in broader multimodal reasoning. The limitation is acknowledged briefly in Section 6, but its implications on generality could be better explored. For instance, how would multi-sentence justifications, chain-of-thought, or open-ended descriptions behave under spurious images?

2) Potential construction bias and limited human verification
- QA pairs are generated by GPT‑4 (Section 2.2 Step1). Only 20% of pairs per category are manually verified (Section C.1; Table 9). The City category has 78% authenticity, which is low given the dataset’s role as a benchmark. Erroneous or ambiguous questions may lead to underestimated model performance or noisy AccDrop measurements. A more extensive human validation pass or resolution of ambiguous items is needed.

3) Confounding factors introduced by typography (OCR) condition
- Typography images are plain white backgrounds with the answer printed (Section 2.2 Step3). This setting may be closer to a lexical conflict test of OCR-plus-language fusion rather than a general visual spuriousness test. The very high AccDrop for many models in Table 3 could be partly due to this construction, which strongly foregrounds the spurious token with minimal competing visual content. The paper should discuss this confound explicitly, since the typography results may not generalize to more realistic scenes containing text-in-context (e.g., signs within images).

4) Metric and matching protocol sensitivity
- The accuracy metric permits approximate matches, including abbreviations and answers embedded in longer sentences (Section 2.3). This is reasonable but can subtly benefit verbose models or penalize concise ones depending on how often near-misses occur. More detail about edge cases and a sensitivity analysis to different normalization/matching schemes would increase confidence in comparisons across diverse model behaviors.

5) Incomplete positioning relative to contemporaneous hallucination/spurious-correlation benchmarks
- The related work focuses on object hallucination and jailbreak papers but omits several highly relevant multimodal hallucination and spurious-bias benchmarks and detection frameworks (see “Potentially Missing Related Work”). This makes it hard to compare CorrelationQA’s task design to other recent proposals that also quantify multimodal spuriousness and hallucination.

Additional but secondary points for authors’ consideration:
- Figure 2 (radar chart) is informative but needs a more explicit legend or textual cross-reference explaining whether it depicts Acc under spurious conditions across categories, and which models trend toward larger lobes; as is, it risks being read as qualitative.
- The “Accuracy Declined Ratio” (ADR) in Section C.3 can be negative (Tables 7 and 8) where AccDrop is negative. It would help to either clamp at zero or report negative cases separately with interpretation, because “declined ratio” semantically suggests non-negativity.
- Table 4 contrasts synthetic vs realistic spurious images only for two models. Expanding to more models, even on a reduced sample, would strengthen the generalizability claim.

Figure/Table-specific critique to support or question claims:
- Figure 5: The gap between “Text-only” and “Spurious” bars for Qwen‑VL and MiniGPT‑4 clearly illustrates the central effect. However, including error bars or reporting variance across random seeds or data subsamples would help disambiguate whether differences are robust.
- Tables 2 and 3: Category-wise AccDrop patterns are consistent with the narrative, but the paper attributes LLaVA’s low AccDrop to exposure to knowledge-heavy datasets like OK‑VQA/A‑OKVQA. This is plausible but speculative; adding a controlled ablation with a smaller LLaVA variant or training logs confirming such data exposure would make the argument more compelling.

## Potentially Missing Related Work
1) Wang et al., “AMBER: An LLM-free Multi-dimensional Benchmark for MLLMs Hallucination Evaluation,” 2023 — Another dedicated hallucination benchmark. Should be discussed in Related Work and compared conceptually in Section 4 and empirically in Section 3.3 if feasible.

2) Ye et al., “MM‑SpuBench: Towards Better Understanding of Spurious Biases in Multimodal LLMs,” 2024 — Directly targets spurious biases in MLLMs with a structured taxonomy. Should be positioned side-by-side in Section 1/2 to clarify differences in task setup and spuriousness modeling, and referenced when interpreting category-wise effects.

3) Chen et al., “Unified Hallucination Detection for Multimodal Large Language Models,” 2024 — Presents UNIHD and MHaluBench; relevant for detection/diagnosis methods that could operate on CorrelationQA. Cite in Section 4 and consider as a post-hoc detector baseline.

4) Qian et al., “How Easy Is It to Fool Your Multimodal LLMs? An Empirical Analysis on Deceptive Prompts,” 2024 — Studies deceptive textual prompts and conflicting modalities. Compare in Section 3.3 discussion of text-only vs multimodal settings and contrast the roles of visual vs textual deception.

5) Gunjal et al., “Detecting and Preventing Hallucinations in Large Vision Language Models,” 2023 — Proposes fine-grained detection and mitigation. Add to Section 4 and consider evaluating a detection baseline on CorrelationQA.

6) Tong et al., “Eyes Wide Shut? Exploring the Visual Shortcomings of Multimodal LLMs,” 2024 — Uses CLIP-blind pairs to expose visual shortcomings, closely related to visually induced hallucinations. Discuss in Section 4 to connect representation-level issues to spurious-image bias.

7) Li et al., “VidHalluc: Evaluating Temporal Hallucinations in Multimodal Large Language Models for Video Understanding,” 2025 — Extends hallucination analysis to video. Mention in Section 6 as a natural extension path.

8) Wang et al., “VideoHallucer: Evaluating Intrinsic and Extrinsic Hallucinations in Large Video–Language Models,” 2024 — Another video benchmark; include in Section 4 and Section 6 to situate CorrelationQA within a broader progression to video.

9) Kogilathota et al., “HALP: Detecting Hallucinations in Vision‑Language Models without Generating a Single Token,” 2026 — Introduces pre-generation probes; relevant for using internal signals to flag high-risk spurious-image inputs. Discuss in Section 3.3 or 4 as potential mitigation applicable to this setting.

10) Gu et al., “MLLMGuard: A Multi‑dimensional Safety Evaluation Suite for Multimodal Large Language Models,” 2024 — Places hallucination within a safety evaluation context; cite in Section 4 and discuss alignment with your safety framing.

## Comments Suggestions And Typos
Actionable suggestions:
- Expand human verification. Increase manual validation beyond 20% and especially address categories with lower authenticity (Table 9). Providing a cleaned subset with certified correctness would improve confidence and facilitate adoption.
- Strengthen typography condition. Consider adding more realistic text-in-scene images (e.g., signs, posters, labels) or mixed-content typography where the spurious token is embedded within clutter. This will help separate OCR legibility effects from genuine spuriousness.
- Report robustness statistics. Add variance or confidence intervals for Acc and AccDrop, at least via bootstrap over items. Figure 5 and Figure 4 would benefit from error bars to convey stability.
- Clarify matching protocol. Provide a stricter normalization scheme and a sensitivity analysis for the approximate-match evaluation (e.g., exact match vs abbreviation vs substring). Include examples that are judged borderline and how they are handled.
- Deepen analysis of training-data influence. The explanation that LLaVA’s low AccDrop arises from exposure to knowledge-heavy datasets is plausible but not verified. If logs are unavailable, at least test an alternative LLaVA checkpoint or a simple instruction-only ablation to see whether ignoring images yields similar accuracy, which would support the “rely on language priors” hypothesis.

Criteria that could raise my score:
- Release of the dataset, code, and evaluation scripts; expanded human verification; strengthened typography realism; and an ablation or auxiliary experiment that substantiates claims about training data effects and the relation to LLaVA’s atypically low AccDrop.

Typos/clarifications:
- Page 1: “Beaf jerky” should be “Beef jerky.”
- Page 6, “Typo+Spu” in Figure 5 legend is slightly ambiguous; consider “Spurious Typography.”
- Ensure consistent model naming (e.g., “Qwen-VL” vs “QwenVL”; “MiniGPT-4.” in Table 3 has a trailing period).
- Page 4: “Successful Answer Rate” section, tighten notation spacing in equations (e.g., Acc = C/T) and variable descriptions.
- Several figure captions could be more self-contained; for Figure 2 specify exactly what the plotted metric is and which models are shown.

## Limitations And Societal Impact
- The paper acknowledges some limitations (Section 6), notably the entity-style answers and lack of exploration beyond images. Additional aspects to discuss:
  - Data provenance and copyright for web-collected images; Stable Diffusion generations may also inherit biases.
  - Dual use: CorrelationQA can be used to craft targeted spurious images to manipulate MLLMs, which has safety implications in deployment.
  - Language and cultural coverage: the benchmark appears English-centric; discuss broader language applicability and whether biases may arise in non-English settings.
  - Potential disparate impact: if models rely heavily on visual cues that correlate spuriously with demographic attributes, harms may arise in assistive or decision-support applications. While this benchmark does not directly target demographic attributes, the general failure mode is relevant.

## Ethical concerns
The dataset includes Internet-scraped images and synthetic images. The paper should explicitly detail licensing, copyright handling, filtering of sensitive content, and any face/PII considerations for realistic images. An explicit ethical considerations section is recommended. Absent that, none of the experiments themselves appear to raise exceptional risks beyond the dual-use concerns noted above.
