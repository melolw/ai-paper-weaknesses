## Paper Summary
The paper proposes CIR3, a multi-agent LLM framework for generating sets of question–answer (QA) pairs from domain-specific, information-dense documents. The system uses: (i) a classifier and moderator to induce multiple subtopic “perspectives,” (ii) a group of writer agents that iteratively reflect and refine QA proposals using a transactive memory, and (iii) a curmudgeon agent, optionally supported by an external diversity tool (Vendi Score), to introduce variation and guide collective convergence. The authors evaluate on financial and insurance passages (FiQA, InsuranceQA V2), comparing against two baselines: a direct LLM prompting baseline and a pipeline where questions are generated by a T5 model and answers by an LLM. They report gains on lexical and embedding metrics, LLM-based evaluation (a G-EVAL variant), a small-scale human study, and ablations showing benefits from perspectives and the curmudgeon component.

## Summary Of Strengths
- Clear articulation of hypotheses H.1–H.3 and a coherent agent topology. Figure 1 effectively communicates the end-to-end workflow, the role of each agent, and how inner/outer refinement cycles write to transactive memory before final termination; this helps readers understand how signals move through the system and when convergence is declared.
- Sound engineering of a multi-stage evaluation suite: lexical metrics (METEOR/ROUGE-L/Jaccard), embedding similarities (BERTScore, BGE), LLM-based scoring adapted from G-EVAL, and a targeted human evaluation with domain experts. Tables 1–3 show improvements across metric families; the combined perspective is more convincing than relying on a single metric type.
- The ablation study isolates two central design decisions. Table 5 and Figure 2 tie the “curmudgeon” and “multi-perspective” elements to both outcome quality (comprehensiveness/faithfulness) and process dynamics (distribution of refinement cycle counts). This directly supports H.2 and, to an extent, H.3.
- The formalization of the task objective, G* = argmax(Comp + Faith), while high-level, neatly frames the target properties. Equations (1)–(6) in Appendix C.2 precisely define how pairwise and concatenated similarities are computed, allowing others to replicate the scoring protocol.
- The paper addresses a difficult setting, open-ended QAG for dense domain texts, with a process aimed at minimizing blind spots and over-collapse. That orientation is valuable for practitioners seeking to generate ECQA-like sets for downstream tasks, fine-tuning, or retrieval augmentation.

## Summary Of Weaknesses
1) Evaluation target and metrics may not measure “comprehensiveness” faithfully enough.
- The paper mainly compares generated questions and answers against the source context rather than a reference QA set. This is understandable, but computing ROUGE/METEOR/Jaccard and even embedding similarity between Q/A and the context (Tables 1–2) risks rewarding lexical copying or semantic proximity rather than true coverage of distinct key points. The method could produce verbose, context-near text and still score well, without necessarily broad coverage. This limitation should be directly acknowledged and mitigated by, for example, coverage proxies (e.g., content-unit extraction from the context and recall over those units), novelty/overlap penalties, or diversity metrics reported across QAs rather than only via the Vendi-assisted curmudgeon.
- While LLM-based scoring (Table 3) aims to assess coverage and faithfulness, it is still model-judged and may reflect evaluator bias. The paper would benefit from an independent rubric with content-unit annotations for at least a subset of documents, allowing a grounded “coverage recall” metric.

2) Limited and somewhat weak baselines.
- The baselines are a single strong LLM with direct prompting (LLM-DP) and a T5-based question generator plus LLM answering (QGEN-AGEN). There are no comparisons to stronger single-agent reflective or debate-style prompting strategies, retrieval-augmented pipelines, or other multi-agent orchestration frameworks of comparable ambition. This makes it hard to isolate how much of the lift comes from multi-agent coordination versus simply better prompting. Concretely, missing baselines include: single-agent multi-round self-reflection, self-consistency sampling of QAs, tree-of-thought or debate prompting, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over the same passages.

3) The “curmudgeon + Vendi” claim is under-validated quantitatively.
- The paper states that the curmudgeon uses Vendi to balance diversity in questions/answers while keeping answer+context closer, but there are no explicit diversity trend plots over iterations or a report of actual Vendi values per iteration/cycle. Figure 2 shows refinement cycle distributions, not diversity/coverage signals. Without reporting diversity metrics longitudinally (e.g., Vendi(Q), Vendi(A), Vendi(A⊕,C)) and correlating them with final quality, H.3 remains partly qualitative.

4) Lack of operational definition and measurement for Comp and Faith within the argmax formalization.
- Section 3 frames an optimization of Comp(G, c) + Faith(G, c), but the paper never concretely defines these functions beyond the evaluation stage. In practice, CIR3 relies on human-designed prompts and a curmudgeon tool to indirectly push toward those properties. It would strengthen the work to define explicit, reproducible Comp/Faith estimators (even imperfect) that are used within the loop, or to connect the Vendi-based and LLM-evaluator signals more explicitly to Comp/Faith surrogates.

5) Human evaluation is limited in scale and methodology details.
- Table 4 uses 80 samples with 8 annotators, but important details are light: inter-annotator agreement statistics, task time, blinding to system identity, and annotation quality controls are not reported. Given that the LLM-based evaluators are already model-judged, the human study needs to be more rigorous to substantiate the main claims.

6) Missing discussion of compute cost and efficiency.
- The system runs up to L=12 inner cycles and K=6 outer cycles, with multiple agents per document. There is no report on token counts, latency, or cost per document. Table 5 and Figure 2 show iteration distributions but do not translate that into concrete cost. Practical deployability, especially at the 2k-document scale per dataset, remains unclear.

7) Homogeneous model family and risk of evaluation bias.
- All agents are from the same Llama-3 family (generation and debate), and while GPT-4 is used for evaluation, there is limited analysis of cross-model robustness. Would a different judge reverse or narrow the gains? How do results change if some writer agents or the curmudgeon use different LLM families?

8) Limited qualitative analysis.
- The paper does not provide example QA sets or error analyses in the main text. Qualitative case studies could clarify what “comprehensiveness” looks like in practice, where CIR3 adds questions that baselines miss, or where the curmudgeon reorients the group.

9) Some positioning gaps with closely related multi-agent QA and RAG literature.
- The Related Work emphasizes multi-agent debate and consensus but omits several directly relevant multi-agent QA or RAG systems and evaluation frameworks that would contextualize the novelty and choices made here.

10) Minor clarity issues.
- In Figure 1, the loop over K and the handoff between inner and outer cycles are depicted, but the “decision” node “Faithful? Comprehensive? Convergence?” would benefit from a precise stopping criterion in the text beyond “curmudgeon is satisfied.” Similarly, Appendix A.1’s pseudocode is helpful but the stopping condition F’=∅ could be reified in terms of measurable thresholds.

Explicit figure/table engagement:
- Figure 1 is a strong architectural diagram, but the decision threshold and the exact moment when the “agreement” status is set are still informal; mapping each arrow to the variables in Algorithm 1 would remove ambiguity.
- Figure 2 supports H.3 only indirectly via iteration counts. To convincingly argue diversity maintenance and avoidance of collapse, add per-interval diversity metrics (e.g., Vendi(Q), Vendi(A), Vendi(A⊕ + context)) or show coverage of extracted content units versus cycle count.
- Table 1 and Table 2 show consistent gains for CIR3 across lexical and embedding metrics, but since these are computed against the context rather than a gold QA set, they should be interpreted as faithfulness/groundedness proxies, not direct measures of comprehensiveness. Table 3 and Table 4 then claim large deltas on LLM and human scoring; stronger baselines and a larger human study would make these improvements more conclusive.
- Equations (1)–(6) precisely define the various averaging and concatenation-based scores; these formal pieces are clear and reproducible even if their relation to “comprehensiveness” is indirect.

## Potentially Missing Related Work

Directly relevant to Section 3 and Figure 1. Should be discussed in Related Work (Section 2) and contrasted empirically or at least conceptually in terms of orchestration and convergence.

2) Sharma et al., Retrieval Augmented Generation for Domain-specific Question Answering (2024) — RAG pipeline for specialized documentation. Given CIR3’s domain-specific goals, discuss in Section 2 and consider as an additional baseline or a hybrid variant, especially relevant to Sections 4.2 and 5.1.

3) Sun et al., Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Domain-Specific Question Answering: A Case Study on Pittsburgh and CMU (2024) — RAG with hybrid annotation/evaluation. Relevant to Section 4.4 and 5.1 for designing stronger baselines and evaluator setups; add to Related Work and experimental discussion.

4) Sun et al., Harnessing Multi-Role Capabilities of Large Language Models for Open-Domain Question Answering (2024) — Single LLM playing multiple roles in a coordinated pipeline. Technically aligned with CIR3’s role specialization; cite in Section 2 and compare the orchestration design in Section 3.1–3.3.

5) Filice et al., Generate but Verify: Answering with Faithfulness in RAG-based Question Answering (2025) — Proposes explicit faithfulness metrics for QA. Highly relevant to Section 4.4 and 5.1; consider adopting their precision/recall-style faithfulness measures and discuss after Table 3.

6) Kumar et al., Automatic Question-Answer Generation for Long-Tail Knowledge (2023) — Automatic QAG for sparse, specialized knowledge. Relevant to CIR3’s motivation in Section 1 and to evaluation choices in Sections 4–5; add to Related Work.

7) Qian et al., Hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning for retrieval-augmented industrial document question answering (2026) — Multi-agent hierarchical coordination for complex, long industrial documents. Useful for future extensions in Section 6; cite in Related Work.

8) Hyeon et al., MATA: Multi-Agent Framework for Reliable and Flexible Table Question Answering (2026) — Multi-agent reliability and flexible scheduling for structured data. Less central but still aligned with multi-agent orchestration; include in Related Work or Future Work as a complementary direction.

9) Omar et al., Chatty-KG: A Multi-Agent AI System for On-Demand Conversational Question Answering over Knowledge Graphs (2026) — Multi-agent QA over KGs; supports broader positioning of multi-agent QA ecosystems. Add to Related Work.

## Comments Suggestions And Typos
Actionable suggestions:
1) Strengthen baselines and ablations.
- Add at least one strong single-agent reflective baseline (e.g., iterative self-critique with the same LLM), a self-consistency multi-sample baseline, and a debate or tree-of-thought baseline. Include a RAG baseline over the same passages to test whether retrieval alone covers much of the gain.
- Report sensitivity to K and L, and agent count M, including cost/latency per document. A cost-quality frontier would make Figure 2 more actionable.

2) Make Comp and Faith operational inside the loop.
- Define Comp and Faith surrogates that the curmudgeon can explicitly optimize (even if noisy): e.g., content-unit recall for Comp and evidence-linked entailment or a faithfulness classifier for Faith. Then report these over iterations and show how they guide termination (the diamond in Figure 1).

3) Quantify diversity and convergence directly.
- Log Vendi scores for questions, answers, and answer+context at each outer iteration K and show trends. Correlate these with final human scores in Table 4. This would substantiate H.3 beyond cycle counts.

4) Improve human study rigor.
- Report inter-annotator agreement, blinding, calibration examples, and time per annotation. Consider expanding beyond 80 documents and add qualitative examples illustrating where CIR3 improved coverage or corrected hallucinations relative to LLM-DP.

5) Add qualitative analyses and failure modes.
- Provide representative QA sets per system, with highlighted content units covered. Include examples where the curmudgeon steers away from premature convergence, and cases where it overcorrects or causes sprawl.

Typos and minor issues:
- Page 4, “as means to introducing variation” → “as a means to introduce variation.”
- Table 1/2 captions: consider clarifying that scores s(c,Q), etc., use the context as reference, and discuss the interpretation caveats.
- Appendix A.1: “Long short-term memory state” label H could be confused with LSTM; consider “central memory state” consistently, as in Section 3.3.
- Footnote markers sometimes use spaced superscripts; unify formatting.
- Figure 6, bullet on diversity: “maximising score_1 and score_3” likely a typo; should be “maximising score_1 and score_2; minimising score_3.”

Criteria that could change my score:
- Adding strong baselines (reflective, debate, RAG) with competitive prompting and showing CIR3 still clearly wins under human evaluation would significantly increase my confidence; I could move to 3.5–4.0.
- Providing iteration-wise diversity/coverage analyses tied to curmudgeon actions and a larger, well-controlled human study would increase Soundness to 3.5–4.

## Limitations And Societal Impact
- Limitations are partially discussed. Additional points to consider: potential evaluator bias from model-based scoring; generalizability beyond finance/insurance; cost and environmental footprint of multi-round multi-agent inference; and risks of generating plausible but misleading QAs if curmudgeon signals underperform.
- Societal impact: in education and finance, low-faithfulness QAs could misinform learners or users. Mitigate via evidence linking, stronger faithfulness checks, and human-in-the-loop review for high-stakes domains.
