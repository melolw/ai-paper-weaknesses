# Expected Review Outcome:

## Paper Summary
The paper proposes canary-controlled adaptive safe-data interleaving to mitigate emergent misalignment (EM) during LLM fine-tuning. Instead of a fixed safe/target mixing ratio, a controller periodically probes the model with a small set of canary prompts, computes an EMA-smoothed risk estimate (Equation 2), and adjusts the interleaving ratio using a threshold policy with hysteresis (Equation 3). Algorithm 1 describes the training loop. On the Security EM benchmark with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the method reportedly reduces General %Misaligned relative to fixed 5% interleaving (Table 1) and exhibits lower cross-seed variance. Ablations (Table 2) suggest that adaptive timing and EMA smoothing matter beyond the average ratio. Figure 1 diagrams the closed-loop, and Figure 2 plots the learned ratio and smoothed risk over training.

## Summary Of Strengths
- Clear formulation of a closed-loop controller: the use of canary prompts for in-training risk estimation paired with EMA smoothing and hysteresis is well motivated and described succinctly via Equations 1–3 and Algorithm 1. Figure 1 nicely clarifies the overall pipeline, including the checkpointing, judge evaluation, smoothing, and threshold updates.
- Empirical gains on a recent EM benchmark: Table 1 shows lower General %Misaligned (5.39% vs 7.15% with fixed 5%) and lower cross-seed variance, while keeping General %Incoherent comparable. This is a compelling headline result for practitioners who already use interleaving.
- Ablative evidence that timing matters: Table 2 indicates that a fixed schedule with identical average p performs worse than the adaptive controller, suggesting the controller’s timing and responsiveness add value beyond just “more safe data.” This tackles a standard confound in such interventions.
- The method is simple, implementation-light, and likely practical to try: periodic probing with a small canary set and a few controller hyperparameters should be easy to integrate into existing SFT pipelines.

## Summary Of Weaknesses
1) Inconsistency between Figure 2 and text/metrics.  
- The text in Section 4.4 says all seeds reach p_max = 0.20 and that average interleaving ratios range from 0.11 to 0.15, and Table 2 lists Avg p_t up to 0.155. However, Figure 2a shows interleaving ratios that appear capped near 0.05 and never approach 0.20. This discrepancy undermines confidence in the claims about controller behavior and compute overhead. Please reconcile these numbers, add units and exact y-axis scaling, and, if necessary, correct either the figure or the text.

2) Compute and data-volume confounds are not fully controlled.  
- Table 1 compares Fixed 5% vs Canary-Controlled with Avg p_t = 0.13, so the adaptive method uses substantially more safe-data steps. While Table 2 tries to control for this with Fixed p = p̄, the fixed baseline there is not compared in Table 1, and its results lack variability estimates. You should include compute- and ratio-matched baselines in the main table (e.g., Fixed 13%, and a few curriculum schedules such as linear warm-up to 20% and cosine decay) with the same number of safe steps and report means ± std across seeds.

3) Small, weakly validated proxy signal.  
- The canary set has M = 20 prompts, evaluated every K = 30 steps, and the canary-general correlation is weak (r = 0.31, Section 4.4). There is no sensitivity analysis on M, K, judge thresholds, or canary composition. Without calibration to human evaluation or an independent judge family, this proxy could be brittle. Please include sensitivity curves over M and K, an alternative judge family, and a small human audit to validate that canary risk movements track actual safety risk.

4) Limited evaluation scope and generalization.  
- Only one EM benchmark is considered (Security EM). The method’s value proposition is broad, but external validity is unclear. At minimum, include an additional EM-inducing dataset or a qualitatively different task (e.g., reward-hacking or deception settings) to support generality, or temper the claims.

5) Judge reliability and statistical reporting.  
- Table 1 uses LLM-judge-based binary criteria, but there are no confidence intervals for the 100-sample estimates, and no analysis of judge self-consistency or inter-judge agreement. Given temperature 1.0 generation and only three seeds, the reported differences might be sensitive. Please add CIs/bootstraps over generations and seeds, and include inter-judge checks or a known-safe automatic classifier to triangulate.

6) Risk thresholding choices lack justification.  
- Equation 1 flags misalignment via judge thresholds (alignment < 30 and coherence > 50) borrowed from prior work, but there is no ablation on these cutoffs. A threshold sweep would test robustness. Also, explain how r_high and r_low were chosen and whether they were tuned on a held-out development set to avoid test leakage.

7) Canary construction and leakage risk are under-specified.  
- The paper does not describe how the canary prompts were created, whether they are disjoint from training/evaluation content families, or whether they inadvertently overlap with WildGuardMix or Security EM distributions. Please detail selection and release the canaries to support reproducibility and to assess overfitting/triggering concerns.

8) Ablation clarity and completeness.  
- Table 2 lacks standard deviations across seeds for ablations, which weakens the causal interpretation. The “Shuffled p_t” variant is underspecified: Is shuffling done per-seed per-run, stratified by phase, or globally? What aspects of “temporal ordering” are preserved or broken by this shuffle? These details matter for interpreting the 9% degradation.

9) Reporting clarity for sample counts and runs.  
- Section 4.1 says “100 samples per prompt” at temperature 1.0 for both general and in-domain prompts, but Table 1 only reports percentages with ± std over seeds, not over sampling replicates. Clarify whether the ± values include sampling variance and provide CIs that reflect both sources.

10) Positioning vs adjacent detection/control methods.  
- The related work cites survey- and EM-specific papers, but omits several directly relevant works on mixing safe/unsafe data dynamics and on zero-label detection/adaptive control. See the “Potentially Missing Related Work” section for concrete additions and where to discuss them.

11) Claims about “4× lower cross-seed variance” are fragile with n=3.  
- With only three seeds, proportional variance claims are not robust. Please report exact variance values, add more seeds, or at least present nonparametric comparisons and CIs.

Figure- and table-specific notes:  
- Figure 1 is informative and supports the proposed mechanism.  
- Figure 2b’s “safe zone” and thresholds give useful intuition, but the absolute values appear inconsistent with the EMA/rate behavior claimed in text; label precise values and units.  
- Table 1 supports the headline improvement, but omits compute-matched fixed baselines.  
- Table 2 is helpful but needs variability and clearer descriptions for each variant.

## Potentially Missing Related Work
1) Zhao et al., “Learning and Forgetting Unsafe Examples in Large Language Models,” 2024 — Studies dynamics of learning unsafe behavior and mitigation via mixing and sequential vs joint training. Highly relevant to the efficacy and scheduling of safe-data interleaving; should be discussed in Section 2.2 and compared experimentally or at least positioned next to Table 1 results.

2) MacDiarmid et al., “Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL,” 2025 — Presents a complementary pathway to EM via reward hacking; relevant as an additional evaluation setting for adaptive controllers. Discuss in Section 2.1 and note as a target for future benchmarks; ideally add a small experiment or caveat in Section 4.

3) Dubiński et al., “Conditional Misalignment: Common Interventions Can Hide Emergent Misalignment Behind Contextual Triggers,” 2026 — Shows that data mixing and post-hoc defenses can hide misalignment under triggers. Important to discuss in Section 2.2 and to consider in Section 4.5; suggests testing your controller against trigger-conditional setups.

4) Parekh et al., “CANARY: Zero-Label Detection of Fine-Tuning Contamination in Language Models,” 2026 — Proposes unlabeled checkpoint auditing via hidden-state geometry; could be compared against your canary probe mechanism in Section 3.2 and potentially integrated as an alternative risk estimator. Cite near Equation 1 and in Section 4.4.
