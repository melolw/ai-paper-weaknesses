## Summary
KL-Time Replay proposes a function-space approach to replay scheduling for LLM continual learning, replacing FOREVER's parameter-space drift signal with KL divergence-based behavioral drift monitoring on anchor sets. The method triggers replay when cumulative function-space drift exceeds thresholds calibrated to an Ebbinghaus-inspired spaced repetition schedule. On a 5-task text classification benchmark (Standard CL), KL-Time achieves performance comparable to FOREVER (OP 0.704 vs 0.708) while producing substantively different scheduling decisions (14.24% trigger divergence). The paper also reveals that KL drift and parameter update norms, while highly correlated within tasks (>0.97), diverge at task boundaries.

An interesting ablation finding shows that current-task anchors outperform the default past-task anchors (OP 0.713 vs 0.704, 37% fewer replay events), suggesting that monitoring drift on current-task data may be a more efficient scheduling signal. The paper is generated by an automated research system, and while the methodology is sound, the contribution is incremental—establishing parity rather than improvement over the strongest baseline.

## Strengths
1. **Clear Methodological Shift:** The proposal to move from parameter-space to function-space drift for replay scheduling is well-motivated. The intuition that parameter updates are a noisy proxy for behavioral forgetting is sound and aligns with known phenomena in overparameterized models.
2. **Empirical Validation & Ablation:** The paper provides a clean comparison against FOREVER and a well-designed ablation study. The correlation analysis (high within-task, moderate full-trajectory) effectively demonstrates that KL drift and parameter norms diverge at task boundaries, providing concrete evidence for the method's distinctiveness.
3. **Surprising Ablation Insight:** The finding that current-task anchors outperform past-task anchors is a valuable empirical observation. It offers a deeper understanding of what drives effective replay scheduling (monitoring current-task learning difficulty vs. past-task forgetting).
4. **Transparent Automated Research Disclaimer:** Acknowledging that the paper is generated by an automated research system and providing code access demonstrates openness and aids reproducibility.
5. **Concise and Structured Presentation:** The paper is tightly written, with clear section roles and a logical flow from problem formulation to empirical validation.

## Weaknesses
1. **Incremental Contribution (Performance Parity):** The core empirical claim is that KL-Time achieves *comparable* performance to FOREVER (0.704 vs 0.708). Establishing parity without demonstrating a statistically significant improvement limits the perceived research value, especially given that the method adds computational overhead (computing KL divergence on an anchor set at each step).
2. **Ablation Result Challenges Core Narrative:** The ablation in Section 4.4 shows that *current-task* anchors significantly outperform the default *past-task* anchors (0.713 vs 0.704) with 37% fewer replay events. This is under-analyzed and creates a tension: if current-task anchors work better, is the paper's main claim (monitoring past-task function-space drift is a better signal) actually validated? The paper treats this as a side note rather than a key insight.
3. **Missing Related Work (MSSR):** The Related Work section omits MSSR (Lu et al., 2026), a very recent method that also uses Ebbinghaus-inspired adaptive replay scheduling for LLMs. Not comparing against or discussing MSSR weakens the novelty positioning.
4. **Lack of Statistical Significance Testing:** The results claim that model-centric approaches "substantially outperform" step-based baselines, but no statistical significance tests (e.g., paired t-test) are reported. Given the standard deviations (±0.014 to ±0.025), differences of ~0.02 may not be significant.
5. **Theoretical Grounding of Gap:** The motivation that "parameter-space metrics may not directly reflect behavioral changes" is intuitive but lacks citation of the literature on parameter redundancy or the decoupling of parameter changes from function changes, leaving it as a heuristic observation rather than a principled motivation.
6. **Equation and Notation Issues:** There are minor but distracting notation issues: $\Delta \theta_{\varepsilon}$ uses epsilon instead of the step index $s$, and Equation (1) has a redundant $\exp(\log(\cdot))$ formulation. The parameter $\theta$ is not explicitly defined as the LoRA adapter weights.

## Key Issues
**1. Ablation Result Undermines Core Narrative (Severity: Major)**
The ablation in Section 4.4 reveals that *current-task* anchors achieve higher performance (OP 0.713) with significantly fewer replay events (18.7) than the default *past-task* anchors (OP 0.704, 29.9 events). This creates a tension with the paper's primary motivation, which focuses on monitoring behavioral drift on *past tasks* to prevent forgetting. If monitoring drift on the *current* task is the more effective signal, the method's claimed innovation (function-space monitoring of past-task drift) is weakened. The authors must either justify why past-task anchors are conceptually preferred despite lower performance, or reframe the contribution around the more effective current-task signal.

**2. Incremental Value vs. Added Computational Overhead (Severity: Major)**
The paper demonstrates performance parity with FOREVER (0.704 vs 0.708). However, KL-Time requires computing KL divergence on an anchor set (up to 256 examples) at every training step, adding forward-pass overhead that is not present in FOREVER's parameter update norm (which is a cheap byproduct of the optimizer). Without quantifying the per-step latency or total training time of KL-Time vs. FOREVER, it is unclear whether the comparable performance justifies the added computational cost.

**3. Limited Evaluation Scope (Severity: Major)**
The empirical validation is restricted to a single 5-task text classification benchmark (Standard CL) using one model (Qwen3-0.6B). Broader validation on larger models (e.g., 7B+), longer task sequences, or diverse downstream settings (e.g., instruction tuning) is needed to substantiate the claim that function-space drift is a generally viable alternative signal for replay scheduling in LLM continual learning.

**4. Missing Comparison with MSSR (Severity: Major)**
The Related Work section omits MSSR (Lu et al., 2026), a very recent method that uses Ebbinghaus-inspired adaptive replay scheduling for LLM continual learning. MSSR's absence leaves a significant gap in the baseline comparisons, as it directly addresses the same scheduling problem with a different mechanism (memory strength estimation).

**5. Undefined Statistical Thresholds and Jargon (Severity: Minor)**
The "10% practical significance threshold" for trigger divergence and the term "Phase-0 gate" lack clear definition or justification. Reviewers cannot assess whether the 14.24% divergence is meaningful without understanding the arbitrary nature of the threshold or the origin of the "Phase-0" terminology (which appears to be internal automated research jargon).

- **P5 (Evidence & Contributions):** Highlight that KL-Time achieves parity with FOREVER while scheduling replay at fundamentally different steps (14.24% divergence), and surprisingly, current-task drift monitoring proves even more efficient.

- **P2:** FOREVER tracks cumulative parameter motion, which accumulates differently from behavioral drift at the exact moments tasks change.
- **P3:** Introduce KL-Time as a mechanism specifically designed to catch these behavioral cliffs at task boundaries.
- **P4:** Present the correlation analysis as the core theoretical justification.
- **P5:** Conclude with the empirical validation showing that capturing this divergence leads to substantively different, yet equally effective, scheduling decisions.

## Abstract Outline (S1-S5)
- **S1 (Problem):** Replay-based continual learning for LLMs requires adaptive scheduling to decide when to revisit past examples and prevent catastrophic forgetting.
- **S2 (Gap):** Existing model-centric schedulers rely on accumulated parameter update norms, which are noisy proxies for actual behavioral changes due to parameter redundancy in large models.
- **S3 (Method):** We propose KL-Time Replay, which triggers replay based on direct function-space drift, computed as the KL divergence between current and reference model predictions on fixed anchor sets.
- **S4 (Result):** On a 5-task benchmark, KL-Time matches the performance of the strongest baseline (OP 0.704 vs 0.708) while making substantively different scheduling decisions (14.24% trigger divergence).
- **S5 (Insight):** Interestingly, an ablation reveals that monitoring drift on *current-task* examples is even more efficient than past-task monitoring, offering a promising new direction for adaptive scheduling.

## Introduction Outline (P1-P5)
- **P1 (Big Picture):** LLMs must continually adapt to new domains. Experience replay is a leading strategy, but fixed step-based scheduling is inefficient.
- **P2 (State-of-the-Art & Gap):** FOREVER improved upon this by aligning replay with an Ebbinghaus-inspired "model time" based on parameter update norms. However, parameter norms measure model *motion*, not model *forgetting*. In the redundant parameter space of LLMs, massive weight changes can leave task outputs entirely unchanged.
- **P3 (Method Intuition):** To address this, we ask: what if we monitored the actual behavioral drift? We introduce KL-Time Replay, which computes the KL divergence of predictions on a fixed set of anchor examples. When the model's behavior drifts beyond an Ebbinghaus-calibrated threshold, replay is triggered.
- **P4 (Evidence Preview):** We validate KL-Time against FOREVER. We show that while the two signals correlate within tasks, they diverge at task boundaries, leading to a 14.24% trigger divergence while maintaining performance parity. We further uncover that current-task drift monitoring is a highly efficient scheduling signal.
- **P5 (Contributions):** (1) The KL-Time Replay algorithm, (2) Empirical validation establishing function-space drift as a viable alternative, (3) An analysis of parameter vs. function signal divergence, and (4) A surprising finding on the efficiency of current-task anchor monitoring.
